What SRE is, and the model behind it

You will learn

The big picture

The name breaks into three parts. Site is the reason SRE exists: users must be able to use the product, so the work ties back to business impact. Reliability is the single thing SREs own, and that narrow focus lets them build expertise generalists can't.

The hierarchy of production health, base first. Grow it wider and taller together: don’t build the top before the base can hold it.

Engineering is the method: you solve operational problems with software and repeatable processes rather than heroics. A quick test for any project is whether it helps the business, improves reliability, and uses an engineering approach.

The book replaces the old Dickerson pyramid (monitoring, incident response, postmortems, testing, capacity, development, product) with a hierarchy of production health: sociotechnical foundations, then situational awareness, continuous preparedness, infrastructure design, resilient products and finally emergent reliability, where the platform makes systems reliable by default.

The old pyramid was mostly reactive and left culture and data integrity implicit. You grow the new one wider and taller at the same time; don't build the top before the base can hold it.

Why the old pyramid was replaced

The first edition used a pyramid drawn by Mikey Dickerson in 2013, modelled on Maslow. Interviewers who read that edition may still have it in mind, so know both and know what changed.

Dickerson’s hierarchy (2013)Hierarchy of production health (2nd edition)
BaseMonitoringSociotechnical foundations: culture and people
ThenIncident response, postmortemsSituational awareness: SLOs, observability, toil tracking, goals
ThenTesting and release, capacity planningContinuous preparedness: incidents, learning, training
ThenDevelopmentInfrastructure design: delivery, testing, data, capacity
TopProductResilient products, then emergent reliability

The book gives four reasons for the redesign. The lower layers were all reactive. Capacity planning had a layer of its own while data integrity was hidden inside “development”. Culture was assumed, not named. And nothing described the end state, where a platform makes services reliable by default.

The inverted pyramidYou don’t have to finish one layer before starting the next. Grow the pyramid wider and taller at the same time. What you must avoid is a wide top on a narrow base: clever self-healing platforms run by a team with no blameless culture and no SLOs.

SRE is a role, not only a skill set

Any team can borrow SRE methods, and DevOps shows that works. The book still argues for a dedicated role, for three reasons.

  • It’s hard to do part-time. Feature deadlines always win the week.
  • It needs depth and breadth. An SRE sees across service boundaries, where most large outages live.
  • Reliability needs its own voice. It is a long-term investment, and the next launch will override it unless someone is accountable for it.

The stance matters as much as the role: SREs are enablers, not gatekeepers.

Three advantages of the software mindset

Objective understandingHealth is measured through SLOs and observability, and processes are derived from first principles: postmortems, STPA, CAST.
AutomationEfficiency and consistency together. It also gives you a reason to simplify, because simple systems are easier to automate.
Dev and ops integratedOne shared language and a feedback loop from production back into design.

That is why every SRE is expected to write code, even if on a given day the code is only a monitoring configuration.

What each upper layer contains

LayerWhat the chapter puts in it
Situational awarenessSLOs and observability, plus two things people forget: toil tracking (no exact number exists, so use outages, ticket throughput and a rough maintenance estimate) and business alignment through OKRs and feature velocity. Without the last one SRE gets sidelined.
Infrastructure designSix areas: release management (changes are the top cause of outages, so use incremental rollouts, trunk-based development and many small releases), configuration management (GitOps, feature flags), data, capacity (load balancing, throttling, DoS protection), production testing (probers, fault injection, load tests) and observability.

AI is missing from the pyramid on purpose. The authors treat it as something that cuts across every layer, which is why it gets its own part at the end of the book.

Say it in an interview

"SRE treats operations as a software problem. That gives you three things: you measure production health objectively with SLOs and observability, you automate for efficiency and for consistency, and you share a language with developers so feedback from production flows back into design. Toil has a ceiling of 50% of an SRE's time. That's a limit, not a goal, and good teams sit far below it."

Situational awareness means CloudWatch and X-Ray; preparedness means Incident Manager and game days; infrastructure design means multi-AZ, CodeDeploy, AWS Backup and FIS; emergent reliability means golden paths through Service Catalog or Control Tower guardrails.

The value of reliability

You will learn
  • how to present SRE as an investment with a return
  • what each stakeholder gets from it
  • the full cost of unreliability, beyond lost sales

The big picture

Present SRE as an investment with a return, not as a cost centre. The headline mechanism is sublinear scaling: by automating and standardising, operating costs grow more slowly than the service does. Traditional ops teams are rewarded for stability and so resist change. SRE uses the error budget so that stability and velocity are traded deliberately and both sides share the goal.

CustomersReliability is the feature every other feature depends on. Not 100%, though: that is always the wrong target.
The CFOSoftware leverage. Code runs again and again at almost no extra cost, and shared platforms spread the gain.
Product teamsObjective targets and less cognitive load.
The SRE teamProtection from burnout, including the right to hand a pager back.

Different stakeholders get different value. Customers get reliability as the feature everything else depends on (but not 100%, which is always the wrong target). The CFO gets software leverage: code runs again and again at almost no extra cost, and shared platforms like one global load balancer spread the gains.

Product teams get objective targets and less cognitive load. The SRE team gets protection from burnout; at Google, SRE can hand the pager back to a team whose service keeps missing its SLO.

The cost of unreliability is more than lost sales. There's churn, the opportunity cost of engineers firefighting, rising toil, slower velocity, expensive retrofits, SLA penalties, and a management fan-out of roughly 100 to 1000 engineer-hours per hour of major downtime once you count reviews, comms and remediation.

Systems also atrophy without investment: small fixes turn into projects, engineers grow afraid to change anything, and the spiral feeds itself.

One hour of downtime, counted properly

The chapter’s most quotable table argues that a major outage costs 100 to 1,000 times its length in engineering time. Roughly, for one hour of serious downtime:

A number worth rememberingGoogle’s research found that 53% of visitors give up on a task when a page takes more than three seconds to load. Slow counts as down.

The startup that deferred everything

A sidebar describes a SaaS company growing revenue 200% a year while putting off basic production hygiene. The bill arrived as twelve months of all-hands catch-up work. Uptime went from 99.84% to 99.999%, which is the difference between about an hour of downtime a month and a few seconds. Six questions before you adopt SRE

  1. What are your biggest operational challenges?
  2. What resources do you have? That includes skills, and whether the culture will fall back to quick fixes.
  3. Can you start small?
  4. What is at stake if you’re unreliable?
  5. How do users interact with you? If they retry, short outages hurt less than long ones. A flash sale may still sell out during a blip and damage your reputation anyway.
  6. Is timing a factor, such as a peak season?

Smaller points that come up

  • Not every Google system has SRE support. The mission-critical ones do, and SRE can hand a pager back.
  • Traditional operations is rewarded for stability, so it resists change. SRE shares an error budget with developers, so both sides own the trade.
  • The CFO’s argument: machines are fast and cheap, people are slower and far more valuable. One global load-balancing platform serves Search, Maps and YouTube.
  • Reliability is a company-wide habit. Sales and marketing should warn engineering before a campaign. Google Meet’s growth during COVID is the example of business growth arriving without warning.
Say it in an interview

"For a startup I'd start with the cheap, high-return practices: automated tests, a CI/CD pipeline, symptom-based alerting, a simple incident process with clear roles and blameless reviews, and lightweight design reviews that look at failure domains, rate limits, rollbacks and data recovery. In a mature organisation, SLOs turn into the tool for deciding where reliability investment goes, and centralised platforms give everyone the same reliable building blocks."

Trends in production: what the cloud changed

You will learn
  • why capacity planning became financial engineering
  • the three decouplings behind modern architecture
  • why RTO and RPO alone can’t describe reliability

The big picture

The cloud swapped physical constraints for financial and logical ones. Capacity planning becomes financial engineering (FinOps): pay for just-in-time elasticity around Black Friday instead of holding idle hardware all year, and trade the stability of long-term commitments against cheap but interruptible Spot.

Compute from locationOrchestrators place workloads; VMs stop being pets
→
Storage from computeWarehouses and query engines over object storage
→
Network from hardwareDefined by software and identity

Three decouplings. Platforms followed the same path: from tickets, to self-service golden paths, towards intent-based systems where you declare a target and the platform reconciles it.

Managed services push the abstraction boundary up, so you stop patching hypervisors and focus on failure modes unique to your business. Open standards (Kubernetes, OpenTelemetry) reduce lock-in.

Troubleshooting now involves shared state: the first question is "is it us or the provider?", so treat providers as partners, use account-specific health dashboards and integrated telemetry, and see their SLA as a floor.

Architecture moved from long-lived VM "pets" to workloads, with three decouplings: compute from location (orchestrators), storage from compute (warehouses over object storage), and networking defined by software and identity (eBPF-style kernel hooks).

Platforms moved from tickets to self-service golden paths and are heading towards intent-based systems: you declare a latency target or a budget and the platform reconciles it. The SRE role shifts accordingly towards setting policy.

The book also argues that RTO and RPO are disaster-recovery metrics that see the world as up or down. They can't express gray failures like "30% slower for 10% of users", and they only start counting after a disaster. User-centric SLOs and error budgets move the work earlier.

Capacity became a finance problem

On-premises, you bought for the peak and the hardware sat idle 51 weeks a year. In the cloud you rent elasticity for Black Friday. The catch is that capital expense turns into operating expense that can swing wildly if nobody watches it. That is the job FinOps does: commitments for the steady base, spot for work that can be interrupted, and tags so every team sees its own bill.

From the 2016 “fortress” to now

Ten years ago the unit was a hand-tended VM on a SAN, with configuration drift and a specialist team per layer. Three decouplings changed that. Separating storage from compute removed what the book calls the preallocation tax: you no longer pay for disks sized for a peak that may never come. Networking became software-defined and driven by identity.

Two forces shape the next decade. One is digital sovereignty: a single service may need to run under ten sets of national rules. The other is autonomous optimisation, where control loops trade performance against energy and cost on their own. Both push SRE away from allocating resources and towards governing policy.

Observability: components to context

  • The single pane of glass is a myth. Use open standards so context flows between tools.
  • Distributed tracing is now a basic primitive, not an advanced extra.
  • Quality over quantity: SLOs are the primary signal, and cardinality needs discipline.
  • Access should be open across teams. Telemetry silos are an antipattern.

Why RTO and RPO fall short

They are binary and reactive, like a stopwatch that only starts after a disaster, and their values often come from history or politics. They can’t describe a gray failure such as “30% slower for 10% of users”. An SLO can.

  • AI at scale. More than 3.2 quadrillion tokens a month by mid-2026, accelerator use up fifteen-fold in two years, and machine learning used to predict hardware failures and tune cooling.

Why aren't RTO and RPO enough as reliability metrics?

Show a model answer

They only describe recovery from a catastrophe, assume a binary up/down state, and measure how good you are at firefighting after the fact. Most real failures are partial: elevated tail latency, one feature broken, one cohort affected.

SLOs on critical user journeys capture that degradation continuously and let you act through the error budget before it becomes an incident. Keep RTO and RPO for data and disaster recovery planning, as Chapter 16 does.

The cultural context of SRE

You will learn
  • the two foundations and seven elements of a reliability culture
  • blame awareness, just culture and Westrum’s three culture types

The big picture

Culture is the foundation layer: practices fail when the environment punishes the behaviour they need. Two fundamentals, focus on the user and reliability as a core value, are supported by seven elements: a data-driven mindset, collaboration and shared responsibility, continuous learning, blamelessness, psychological safety, trust and empowerment.

LearningTraining builds skill
→
ConfidencePeople believe they can do it
→
BehaviourThey do it, repeatedly
→
CultureIt becomes how things are done

You change culture by changing what people do. Their thinking follows.

Being data-driven applies to ops health as well as services: track the share of unactionable pages (noisy monitoring), the share of incidents first noticed by humans (wrong SLIs or gaps in coverage), pages per incident, and on-call hours.

Avoid heroics. They hide systemic problems, set unrealistic expectations, burn people out and create knowledge silos. Blamelessness moves the focus from people to systems; blame awareness goes further and asks why the action made sense at the time.

Neither means zero accountability: a "just culture" separates honest mistakes and at-risk behaviour from reckless or criminal conduct.

DORA research ties culture to delivery performance. Westrum's typology sorts organisations into pathological (messengers punished, failure leads to scapegoating), bureaucratic (messengers neglected, failure leads to "justice") and generative (messengers trained, failure leads to inquiry), and generative cultures predict better outcomes.

You change culture by changing behaviour: learning builds confidence, confidence changes behaviour, and repeated behaviour becomes culture. Measure it with surveys on a grounded confidence scale before training, after it, and at one and six months.

Adopting SRE in practice

Brownfield: educate stakeholders together, pick a service that matters with an engaged team and code access, gather baseline SLIs over a few business cycles, have the crucial conversation ("is our current reliability good enough, and what does slow or down cost us?"), set two or three simple, realistic, agreed SLOs, introduce blameless postmortems, then publicise the wins.

Greenfield: treat SLOs as a design input that shapes architecture, build instrumentation in as a feature, and have CI/CD with rollback and runbooks from day one. Then let error budgets gate deployments instead of a fixed release calendar.

Seven elements, two foundations

The chapter rests everything on two foundations: put the user first (which is not the same as reliability at any cost) and treat reliability as a core value that's rewarded like a feature. On top sit seven cultural elements: a data-driven mindset, collaboration and shared responsibility, continuous learning, blamelessness, psychological safety, trust and empowerment.

If you're asked "what does a good SRE culture look like?", list the two foundations first and then give one concrete practice per element.

Data-driven means measuring the process too

Google noticed postmortems were being written late and their action items left open, so it put SLOs on the postmortem process itself: a maximum time to the first draft and to publication, with dashboards and reports to management.

Publication got faster, more action items closed, and repeat incidents with the same root cause dropped. Three incident-health metrics come up repeatedly: the share of incidents that weren't actionable (noisy monitoring), the share detected manually by users or other teams (weak SLIs or coverage) and pages per incident (an on-caller drowning in alerts).

On the people side they track on-call time and how often on-callers make manual production changes, aiming for nearly zero.

Blame awareness and just culture

Blamelessness is the default, but the book goes further with blame awareness: admit that blaming is a natural reflex, then ask why the action made sense to the person given what they knew and the pressure they were under. Watch for the fundamental attribution error (others' mistakes are character flaws, ours are circumstances) and hindsight bias.

Blameless doesn't mean nobody is accountable. Postmortems still check whether engineering standards were met. For regulated industries that worry about malice, the just culture model separates honest mistakes, at-risk behaviour, reckless conduct and criminal acts.

Only the last two justify discipline, and even then you still ask what least-privilege, auditing or approval control should have made the act impossible.

Trust, safety, empowerment

Psychological safety (Amy Edmondson's definition) is what makes people escalate early and ask awkward questions like "have we ever tested a restore?". Managers build it by admitting what they don't understand and by not reacting with feigned surprise to questions.

Repairing trust after one tense exchange takes roughly five to seven positive ones, which is why distributed teams invest in meeting in person. Empowerment at Google means any engineer can propose a code change anywhere, any team can request a postmortem (near misses included) and assign action items to other teams, and SRE can push back on launches or, in extreme cases, hand the pager back.

Learning → confidence → behaviour → culture

You change culture by changing what people do; their thinking follows. Training builds confidence, and you can measure culture cheaply by asking people to rate their confidence in action-oriented tasks on a grounded scale: no idea, could do it with help, could work it out from docs, could do it from memory, could teach it.

Survey before training, straight after, at one month and at six. People who went through SRE onboarding scored 15 to 49 percentage points higher on top-box confidence than those who did general onboarding. When you design training, think about three groups: the change-curious, the change resisters ("what's in it for me?") and change champions (experienced hires who can tell success stories).

Brownfield and greenfield

For an existing service, the sequence is: train all stakeholders together, pick one service that matters and has an engaged team and code access, assess its maturity, collect a baseline SLI on a critical user journey, then hold the crucial conversation with the business: is today's reliability good enough, and what does slow, broken or down cost us?

Engineers tend to underestimate what's needed and business stakeholders tend to overestimate it. Set two or three realistic SLOs (if you're at 99.5% today, don't promise 99.999%), start blameless postmortems, then publicise the wins.

For a new service, the SLO is a design input: 99.99% pushes you away from single-homed designs and towards redundancy, idempotent operations for retries, instrumentation built in as a feature, and load and chaos testing. Swap fixed-cadence releases ("every other Tuesday") for SLO-gated ones.

On AWSCulture has no service, but you can make it visible: publish SLO dashboards widely, use Incident Manager's post-incident analysis templates, and track action-item closure. DORA metrics can be computed from CodePipeline/CodeDeploy events.

Centralised, decentralised or hybrid

A centralised model concentrates expertise and keeps practice consistent. It's the natural start in low-trust, high-control organisations, but it risks a transactional "IT services" mindset and slow onboarding. A decentralised model gives deep context, fast feedback and real ownership, but it risks an us-versus-them split and the role being quietly redefined or dropped.

Trust has to run both ways. Google is centralised but uses hybrid mechanisms: a community of practice, written engagement models, and a dedicated SRE technical programme manager role. Let practice drift too far and you get "shadow SRE".

Reorganisations cost trust and context, and the organisation dips before it improves (a J-curve), so roll changes out gradually with first-mover teams and metrics.

Spotify: ownership without a central SRE org

Teams operate what they build ("Ops in Squads", now "Ops in Areas"). A platform group supplies standard stacks, observability defaults (dashboards and alerts come bundled with the service framework) and automation. A small IMOC (Incident Manager On-Call) rotation of senior engineers follows the sun.

It owns no services, doesn't debug and doesn't take over mitigation. It's engaged only when coordination is the bottleneck: unclear ownership, incidents spanning teams, or cross-cutting decisions such as rerouting traffic or contacting external parties.

Two lessons: distributed ownership only scales once operational variance is reduced through standardisation, and IMOC must not become a quiet way to hand off accountability.

Team design numbers

A lone SRE is a single point of failure; without the headcount for a team, prefer a DevOps model. Every Google SRE team has a leadership-approved charter (Appendix B): mission, scope as a connected whole, services, and success criteria as time-bound outcomes.

Limits: at least 50% engineering time, at most 50% operational work, at most 25% on-call. Two sites 6–9 hours apart allow follow-the-sun, with at least six engineers per site (two to three on-call weeks a quarter each). A single site needs at least eight.

Split a team once it reaches twice its minimum size. Ideally the current on-caller absorbs all the ops work, about 15% of team capacity. Meet key partners in person at least once a year to pay down "relationship debt".

Working with developers

Every Google SRE team has at least one active development partner, often more than ten. Share on-call: developers cover business hours with SRE as escalation, and SRE covers nights with developers as escalation. If SRE alone is primary, developers keep a 24×7 secondary rotation.

Keep a shared backlog and give work to whichever team can do it best. Internally, "look left and right" before building something custom. Managers can't block transfers.

The weekly production meeting

It's about services, not people's status updates. The big picture

How Google’s engagement model evolved.

Archetypes: kitchen sink (often a renamed ops team, reactive), embedded, product/application (easy to show value), infrastructure (effectively platform engineering), tools, consulting, and blended.

Google moved through three models. First, a binary "SRE-supported or not" gated by production readiness reviews: SLOs, less paging, docs, progressive rollouts, backups, capacity plans, failure domains. That was thorough but slow and "sticky".

Next came baseline / assisted / full tiers, which were still centred on who held the pager. Since 2025 it has been outcome-focused: every service in a partnership gets foundational offerings, and SRE and dev agree concrete outcomes with rough timelines, such as faster detection and mitigation, an exercised DR plan, lower operational cost, or faster releases and rollbacks. Consumer and enterprise products differ. Consumer SLOs work well because volume smooths the signal; enterprise customers rely on implicit guarantees, one large customer's complaint can matter as much as an outage, and mitigations have to respect each customer's risk tolerance.

Organisation structure vs engagement model

Org structure answers who pays and who manages. The engagement model is the contract of work: does SRE consult, build, or carry the pager? You can judge an engagement's maturity on three axes: intent (reactive, then specialised, then integrated), impact (depth-first or breadth-first) and value (tactical, then optimised, then strategic, the last asking "how do we make launches succeed?").

Scoping archetypes

  • Kitchen sink: everything SRE. The most common archetype, often an ops team that has just been renamed.
  • Embedded: depth-first, but risks isolation.
  • Product or application: business-aligned, but infrastructure gets duplicated without a platform.
  • Infrastructure: in its modern form, essentially a platform engineering team.
  • Tools: risks solving the wrong problems.
  • Consulting: office hours and PRRs, but advisers can lack context.
  • Blended: a central function, embedded SREs for young products, product SRE for critical ones, infrastructure SRE publishing infrastructure SLOs, shared onboarding and a chief reliability officer.

Monoliths pull you towards product teams; uniform microservices pull you towards infrastructure teams. Re-scope regularly.

Consumer vs enterprise

Consumer products have millions of users, so you lean on metrics, SLOs, error budgets and even sentiment from search trends, and incident response is mitigate first, ask questions later. Enterprise products have concentrated revenue and contracts, so one big customer's broken workload can matter even within SLO.

Google's three engagement models

1. PRR gate (the original). A service was either SRE-supported or "you build it, you run it". The SRE mission: meet availability targets; subject to that, maximise long-term feature velocity; use software instead of toil; and engage only where SRE is more efficient than developers.

Funding came through headcount transfers, which pushed developers to build simpler services. A PRR took quarters and typically produced SLOs, lower pager load, documentation, progressive rollouts, backup plans, capacity plans and failure domains. The problems: engagements became sticky, knowledge stayed with SRE, and SRE arrived late in the lifecycle.

2. Tiers. Baseline (consulting and office hours, no funding), assisted (projects, embedded SREs, maybe shared on-call) and full. It failed because moving between tiers was as painful as onboarding, nobody wanted to give up a tier they'd "earned", and everything still revolved around who holds the pager.

3. Outcomes (2025). Funding is negotiated once per product-area partnership. Foundational offerings go to every service: postmortem reviews, office hours, an incident response team. Everything else is an outcome delivered through a few time-boxed engineering efforts, ranked by business priority and staffed until capacity runs out.

Examples: cut operational load by x%, define and defend user-centric SLOs, build a multi-failure-domain architecture, a data integrity and restore plan. On-call exists to gather information for that engineering work. If the same outcome keeps recurring across partnerships, it becomes an infrastructure team or platform feature.

Case studies

Persistent Disk had SREs semi-embedded in feature teams, overloaded tech leads, unclear ownership (developers assumed SRE owned "all monitoring") and low morale.

Leadership agreed foundational offerings (incident response, product SLO alerts, ownership of expanding into new regions) and four outcomes (faster detect-to-mitigate, an exercised disaster recovery plan, lower ops cost, faster releases and rollbacks).

They regrouped into four teams, decoupled on-call rotations from team structure and wrote OKRs as progress on outcomes. SRE also built self-service alerting so developers no longer depended on them.

Microsoft Azure grew from about 40 SREs in 2015 to about 200.

It uses a written playbook with six sequential phases, each with KPIs: service fundamentals; service health and safe deployment practices (critical user journeys, SLOs, phased rollout of code, config and data); operational efficiency (time-to-x metrics measured at P75); release management (three of the four DORA metrics); risk reduction through threat modelling; and scalability.

Proven solutions become reusable "SRE patterns". The team scales through platforms, horizontal teams (Service Health, Change) and shift-left standards. Its strongest evidence wasn't dollar ROI but the fact that SRE-supported services improved faster on division quality KPIs.

Disney runs a central team embedded long-term: engagements pivot rather than end, with lightweight agreements (what outcome, what risk, what's reliable enough, how it's measured). Proximity builds empathy, empathy builds trust, and trust speeds things up.

On-call is treated as a signal of system health, and models vary by domain (theme-park systems hand over to park engineering after launch). Their hard lesson: when new business leadership no longer wanted modern practices, staying on burned people out. Their rule: don't go where you aren't invited, and leave when the invitation lapses.

Beyond PRRs

Building the engagement around on-call is an antipattern, and so is SRE "holding the pager" after developers move on; hand services back with a plan. PRRs gatekeep, cost effort that grows linearly with launches, and arrive too late.

Replace them with opinionated platforms, standard rollouts, horizontal regression monitoring and automatic rollback, keeping deep reviews for the riskiest launches. Scale influence by guiding (office hours), sharing knowledge (don't be the "human abstraction layer for production") and reducing the need for SRE (standard frameworks, automated policy).

Bring product managers in: they should own the definition of SLOs and hear about user impact regularly. An SLO of 99.99% under 10 ms needs a very different design from 99% under 250 ms.

An SLA is different. It is the agreement about what happens, usually money, when a target is missed, and it is normally looser than the internal SLO.

A critical user journey (CUJ) is a task the user cares about, such as logging in or checking out. It usually spans several services, which is why SLOs written only by backend teams miss things.

Choosing your first SLO

  • Measure the happy path at the load balancer. It is the simplest place to see what users see.
  • Pick an availability target. 99% is relaxed, 99.9% is the common choice and 99.99% is demanding.
  • Pick a goodness threshold, usually latency. Around 100 ms feels instant; over a second feels clunky. A latency SLO is the percentage of requests faster than the threshold.
  • Set the target as a group. Business, product, engineering and operations decide together, because the target is a business decision about user experience.
  • Keep it small. Fewer than five high-level SLOs usually describe a service.

What the second edition adds

  • Adoption pitfalls. Tie the programme to a business reason, iterate, share credit, and give teams templates and SRE help instead of bare mandates. “Eat from both ends”: start with end-user SLOs and core components.
  • Critical user journeys. A synthetic probe must exercise the whole journey. In one case a single 99.99% probe stayed green while small combinations of protocol and destination were completely broken.
  • Per-customer SLOs for B2B, where revenue follows a Pareto curve. An aggregate burn of 10× could be one customer at 100× and nine at zero.
  • Implicit SLOs. Users depend on whatever you have actually delivered (Hyrum’s law). Raising the level wastes the workarounds they built and invites more load; lowering it pushes your costs onto them. Be explicit and over-communicate.
  • Client-side SLOs have a high noise floor. Use bad minutes: a minute is bad if its error rate is above, say, 6% when the noise is 5%. Use sessionised SLIs where there is no denominator.
  • Tooling. Pick one SLO tool and generate the alerts from it automatically.
  • Labels record ownership and lifecycle: experimental, aspirational, committed, deprecated.
  • Failure concentration. Use entropy or decision trees to find the cohort that a single number hides.

Where SLOs fall short

  • They assume countable success and failure events.
  • Nines aren’t intuitive to people.
  • The response is binary: in budget or out.
  • A budget blown in January still needs a monthly refresh, plus a longer programme to fix the cause.

Monitoring versus observability

Traditional monitoring depended on tribal knowledge and on engineers correlating dashboards by hand. It answers questions you already knew to ask.

Observability combines time series, logs (including wide structured events), traces and continuous profiles so that someone without deep prior knowledge can test and reject hypotheses quickly, without shipping new code.

LatencyHow long requests take
TrafficHow much demand there is
ErrorsHow many requests fail
SaturationHow full the system is

The four golden signals are the starting point. RED (rate, errors, duration) suits services; USE (utilisation, saturation, errors) suits resources.

The metric that matters most is time to mitigate, from customer-centric detection to containment, followed by time to detect. The trend is towards knowledge graphs of assets and dependencies, AI-assisted investigation and views centred on customers instead of services.

OpenTelemetry

OpenTelemetry grew out of Google’s Census. Its principles are open, extensible, correlatable, universal and loosely coupled. Its parts are the OTLP protocol, the Collector, the APIs and zero-code instrumentation. It joins signals together in three ways:

ResourceWhere it ran: service, zone, instance.

Instrumentation scopeWhich code: the library, plus stack traces.
A ten-year decisionTreat the choice of an observability product as something you will live with for a decade; the chapter’s Waze migration story shows why. eBPF lets you observe without changing the application at all.

What only clients can see

Client telemetry reveals what backends can’t: DNS and load-balancer problems, expired certificates, failures hidden by retries, and crashes. It has to respect battery, network and privacy, and it must count the data it loses.

Customer-centric monitoring builds critical user journeys out of critical user interactions, and passes the journey ID through the RPC side channel so every hop can label its metrics.

Change, retention and privacy

  • Change supervision covers binaries, config, mobile releases, feature ramps and data pushes, not just code deploys.
  • Retention follows value. Google keeps minute-level data in memory for hours, on disk for weeks, and as 30-minute rollups for years.
  • Privacy needs data classification, minimisation, regional locality, deletion, fine-grained access control and customer-managed keys. Keep user IDs out of metric labels.

The statistics worth knowing

  • Paging on low traffic: page when the lower confidence bound is above the SLO’s error rate.
  • Composing dependencies: in series, success multiplies (80% × 90% = 72%). With hedging, failure multiplies (1 − 0.2 × 0.1 = 98%). Both assume the failures are independent.
  • Latency regressions: build per-cohort baselines, compute z-scores and watch the share of requests above 2. A jump means a regression.

Alerting on evidence, not on a single ratio

One error in twenty requests is a 5% error rate, and it is almost certainly noise. For latency, compare against the service’s own past: group requests into cohorts by method, customer and workload shape, keep a 30-day mean and standard deviation, and alert on z-scores. “Slower than usual” is an implicit SLO that works across products.

Incident management and on-call

You will learn
  • the IMAG roles and how an incident moves through its lifecycle
  • why you mitigate before you understand, and the five generic mitigations
  • how to design on-call that people can sustain
  • what to measure instead of MTTR

The big picture

Define your terms. An incident means users are having a bad time, important properties are degraded, or an outage is happening or imminent; an outage means a service or critical feature can't do its job. Anyone may declare an incident or escalate it, and false positives get relabelled later, which keeps politics out. On-call is a mechanism for coordinating urgent work; paging a whole team triggers the bystander effect.

Incident CommanderHolds every role until they delegate it. Directs, and in big incidents takes no hands-on tasks.
Communications LeadKeeps stakeholders and customers informed on a fixed cadence. May split into internal and external.
Operations LeadRuns the hands-on response: investigating and applying mitigations.

Rotation design has six inputs:

  • Load: roughly two incidents per shift at most. Overload and underload both degrade response.
  • Coverage that matches users: business hours is “on duty”, daylight across two sites gives about 21 hours, and follow-the-sun gives 24×7.
  • Response time: three minutes or less needs two people; 30 minutes allows normal life.
  • System properties that buy slack, such as graceful degradation.
  • Customer constraints, like where responders must be located.
  • Labour law.

Incident response teams coordinate incidents that span many teams. Large organisations run a global IRT plus product-area IRTs with clear escalation triggers and one common command framework; small ones keep a volunteer pool with "inversion of authority" and avoid incident theatre (as large as necessary, as small as possible). Watch for coordination overhead, handoffs, late engagement and skill atrophy.

The incident lifecycle has six stages:

  1. Detect.
  2. Triage. How many users, global or local, which journeys? Declare early. Success means fast, accurate classification and escalation.
  3. Investigate. Root cause analysis is slow and political, and OODA is still linear, so Google prefers multiple hypotheses tested in parallel by trying to disprove them.
  4. Mitigate. Generic, safe mitigations come first: rollback, more capacity, restart, drain. A mitigation is also a diagnostic experiment, and whether you can mitigate is an architectural property.
  5. Resolve. Causes understood, or the change rolled back and blocked, followed by a “wait and see” period.
  6. Learn.

AI in incident response: start with detection and triage (the worst case is over-escalation), summaries, dynamic dashboards and hypotheses. Allow production changes only with guardrails, where each change is reversible and atomic (action, validation and automatic rollback in one audited transaction).

Classify before you respond

Bugs, incidents and outages need different behaviour (immediate engagement versus a ticket). Watch the politics in both directions: if leadership counts outages, people under-label; if declaring an outage lets you bypass policy, people over-escalate. Notifying a whole team invites the bystander effect, while relying on one person creates a single point of failure.

On-call design

Aim for no more than two incidents per shift, assuming each takes four to six hours end to end. A service that almost never pages may not justify SRE at all. Schedule options include business hours, daytime (6am–8pm), a daylight span across sites of about 21 hours, and 24-hour coverage via follow-the-sun or compensatory time.

Required response time drives staffing: about three minutes needs at least two engineers ready to act; thirty minutes or more may not need a laptop at all. Constraints such as data residency, citizenship or security clearance matter, and Google uses a constraint solver for fair rotations. Relax requirements before you burn people out.

IMAG and IRTs

The incident commander holds every role until they delegate one, and comms may split into internal and external leads. Incidents can move between states in any order, and big ones become a tree of child incidents under a parent that waits until all the children are resolved.

Incident response teams are paged directly by criteria. Large organisations run federated IRTs (Google has Tech IRT plus Cloud, Search and YouTube IRTs, all on the same framework). Small organisations use a swarm of senior volunteers per incident.

Google found consensus-driven and executive-as-IC models slower and more stressful. Make business context queryable ahead of time: which customers are critical, which racks hold whose workloads.

Triage and mitigation

Favour data about user experience over whatever errors are easiest to see. Don't penalise false-positive declarations, but remember investigation isn't free, so curate dashboards. Risk tolerance scales with impact: a widely visible outage justifies a riskier mitigation, while a minor one calls for caution, and standing down quickly after triage counts as a win.

Mitigate first, and never blame a reasonable mitigation that didn't work; that's outcome bias ("resulting"). Weigh any action against the risk of doing nothing. OODA has a weakness here: it's hard to tell whether your action caused the change or it was coincidence.

Coordination costs

Humans are poor at passive monitoring beyond about 30 minutes. Assume a responder who has had no question, input or task for an hour has disengaged. Tens or hundreds of responders means you need better observability or training. MTTR averages mislead, because a rollback takes minutes and a physical repair takes days.

Track coordination instead: ramp-up time for new joiners, how often status interrupts work, and the number of channels and tools. In big incidents the IC directs and shouldn't pick up tasks. Learning from incidents

You will learn

  • the safety-science models interviewers expect you to know

The big picture

An outage is an unplanned investment, and the postmortem collects the return. Foundations: put learning first (timeline, impact, contributing causes, lessons), stay blameless, collaborate across teams, and have executives model the behaviour and give it visibility.

In practice: study the incident thoroughly to find the earliest point it could have been detected, ask open questions without judgment, don't let temporary fixes become permanent, give every action item an owner and close it out, and share widely (weekly outage digests, a company-wide newsletter).

What has changed in a decade: safety science and systems thinking (STPA and CAST), storytelling, analysis of communication logs and responders' mental models, an independent postmortem lead who wasn't in the response, accountability that keeps the people closest to the decisions involved in fixing them, customer-centric impact analysis, and layered reviews at peer, team, organisation and executive level.

Rasmussen’s boundaries, drawn

Of the safety-science models in this chapter, Rasmussen’s is the one that needs a picture. A team works inside a space with three edges. Go past the first and the business fails. Go past the second and people burn out. Go past the third and you have an accident.

This explains “drift into failure”. Nobody chose to be unsafe. Each shortcut was a sensible answer to cost or workload pressure, and each one that didn’t cause an outage looked like proof it was fine. The counter-force has to be deliberate and continuous: error budgets, game days, postmortem follow-through. A poster campaign after an outage pushes back for a week.

Safety-science models to name-drop correctly

  • Swiss cheese (Reason): active and latent holes line up, the slices are both technical and human, and near misses are the cases where only one slice held, so ask where you got lucky.
  • Rasmussen: economic and workload pressures push operations towards the edge of acceptable performance, so you need continuous "fire prevention".

The big picture

Some major outages happen without any single component failing, as when a large Maps data update for Japan broke search. The chain-of-events model ("the root cause was the bug") assumes that sequence means causation and stops at a simple story.

1 · Losses and hazardsWhat must never happen, and the states that lead there
→
2 · Control structureWho controls what, and what feedback they get
→
3 · Unsafe control actionsMissing, wrong, mistimed, or too long or short
→
4 · Causal scenariosWhy a controller would do that

STPA looks forward at a design. CAST uses the same model to look back at an incident.

STAMP says accidents happen when the system's design fails to constrain its components' behaviour: missing tests, code too complex to review, global rollouts and aggressive deadlines are why a risky release looked reasonable.

STPA looks forward, and you can run it on a design doc. It has four steps:

  1. Define losses and hazards: detectable states that lead to a loss in the worst case.
  2. Model the control structure: controllers, control actions going down, feedback coming up, and each controller’s process or mental model.
  3. List unsafe control actions: not taken when needed, taken when unsafe, taken too early, too late or in the wrong order, or stopped too soon or applied too long.
  4. Build causal scenarios for each.

CAST looks backward at an incident. It has five steps:

  1. Basic information: losses, hazards, timeline, questions.
  2. The control structure as it was.
  3. Each controller’s contribution, described neutrally (no “should have”), with its mental-model gaps and context.
  4. Flaws in the control structure through systemic factors: safety culture, communication and coordination, management of change, safety information, and changes in the environment.
  5. An improvement plan that traces back to those factors.

Read the picture as four places to look for trouble: the control action can be wrong or late, the feedback can be missing or misleading, the controller’s mental model can be out of date, and the process can behave in a way nobody modelled.

Why safety engineering

A 2019 Google Maps postmortem was well written, yet leaders felt it was insufficient, because no component had failed. Chain-of-events models are about a century old, and picking a "root cause" is arbitrary, since you can always go further back.

A better question is whether the fix would prevent similar incidents, not just this one. Safety is an emergent property: you can't read it from one component's spec.

Doing STPA well

Losses include "loss of mission" (a user can't navigate) as well as revenue and trust. A hazard is a state, for example a published map that doesn't match the real world. Build a functional control structure, not a structural one: five teams that can deploy become one "deploys updates" controller.

Google's diagrams usually have 5–10 boxes, roughly a tenth the size of a dataflow diagram, and drawing one together gives teams a shared language. Every controller has responsibilities, a control algorithm and a process model (for software) or mental model (for humans), and those models are only as good as the feedback is correct, complete and timely.

To find causal scenarios, ask: was feedback missing or wrong, was the model inaccurate, was a safe action overridden or lost in communication, or did the system react unexpectedly?

The big picture

You automate to save time in normal operations and in emergencies, and for consistency and scale. Automation moves through levels: none, then partial (where teams can get stuck), then full, which can be local, platform-wide, or best of all inherent system behaviour that removes the task altogether.

The safety ladder for new automation. Move up one rung at a time, and the whole team can follow along.

Capacity provisioning shows the progression: hardcoded replicas → autoscaling across locations → sizing by customer usage → continuously optimised placement, run by two layers of platform automation (minutes to hours, and days to months).

Bainbridge's ironies of automation: it creates new work (monitoring and fixing the automation), erodes operators' skills, and leaves humans the hardest cases. The pitfalls are dangerous automation without safety checks, deskilling, automation surprise, automating over a broken foundation (auto-restarting a memory leak), automating a moving target, and automating ambiguous decisions.

To decide, combine quantifiable costs and benefits (engineers underestimate maintenance) with strategic value, hidden risk costs, ambiguity and the human annoyance of the toil. Build up incrementally (the automation snowball of quick wins first earns trust), add extra safety to early versions, and don't stop at 90%. The Traffic Director and Pod story shows configuration sprawl turning into cascading failures.

Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, without lasting value, and grows linearly with the service. It isn't just "work I dislike".

Measure it, keep it below 50% (a ceiling), reward fixing whole classes of problems, spot procedural causes (a team that just forwards tickets), and avoid the false fixes: declaring an emergency, more spreadsheets, ignoring it, appointing a "toil expert", outsourcing it, or handing it to the newest person.

Toil is acceptable during mitigation, while learning a process, or where human judgment is needed. It isn't acceptable when it plugs a product gap, grows with the system, or exists only because access is restricted; build narrow, least-privilege tools for that instead.

Why and how far to automate

There are four reasons to automate: saving time in normal operations, saving time in emergencies (cutting a Cyber Monday outage from 15 minutes to 5 beats shaving an hour off weekly qualification), consistency (especially for emergency actions that need elevated privileges) and scale.

The levels run from none, to partial (where teams can get stuck), to full, which can be local, platform-wide or, best of all, inherent behaviour (for example, a sync job disappears once there's one source of truth).

Capacity provisioning shows the path: a hard-coded prototype becomes platform defaults until GA, then automated planning with two independent layers (minutes-to-hours autoscaling and days-to-months forecasting). Pick the abstraction carefully, letting users choose mean, P90 or peak load rather than tuning forecasts themselves.

Pitfalls and the ironies

Bainbridge's ironies of automation: automation creates new and harder work (watching it and repairing it), erodes operators' skills, and so demands more skilled people.

The other traps are dangerous automation without rate limits, scope limits, do-no-harm checks and circuit breakers; deskilling (the 99% case); automation surprise; automating over a broken foundation; automating a moving target; and automating ambiguity.

Deciding

Run the cost-benefit numbers (and assume you've underestimated the costs), then weigh the things you can't quantify: strategic value (patching a fleet fast), the cost of the safety mechanisms themselves, the ambiguity factor, and the annoyance factor (a five-minute task that wakes someone at night).

In Google's network fault mitigation case, speed dominated, but a bad signal could drain healthy infrastructure, so it needed a safety system ("never drain more than X% of a region"). Over time those overlapping checks became hard to reason about and started blocking legitimate repairs.

Standardising deployment on Pod changed the economics: once regionalisation came built in, hundreds of low- and medium-risk services adopted it. Restarting a process with a memory leak is fine if the leak is rare and expensive to fix, but it becomes debt if the restarts outpace your release cadence.

Incremental automation

"See one, do one, automate one." Use the production meeting to pick candidates, then go from manual, to scripts a human triggers, to fully integrated. An early safety ladder: the automation first only detects and alerts, then suggests an action, then acts and notifies, so the whole team can follow along.

Prioritise the fastest wins first to start an automation snowball. Don't stop at 90%, because half-automated processes confuse people and add cognitive load; give the automation readable logs and dashboards with linkable events. Make sure defaults never act on everything: outages have happened when a missing variable such as the region was treated as "all".

Traffic Director

Google chose multi-single-tenant (one instance per tenant) for velocity and to limit each tenant's blast radius, and Pod's single config made complexity grow sublinearly for a while. Eventually it became the largest config at Google.

Per-tenant flags diverged, rollouts got stuck and production ran a two-month-old binary. The postmortem's "Blood and Toil" section described hundreds of manual operations per release. After an 18-month joint stabilisation effort with the platform team, the service moved to multi-tenancy. The lesson: revisiting an architecture that served well for five years is a sign of maturity, not of failure.

Toil

Toil is manual, repetitive, scales linearly and leaves nothing lasting behind. Hard engineering work, like a new security policy or tests after an outage, isn't toil. Measure toil, and track the ratio of actionable to noisy alerts.

Keep engineering above 50% (for example, reduce toil from 50% to 30%). Catch toil at launch with a checklist, and notice the pages you should have received. Reward fixing whole classes of problems, through peer bonuses and promotion criteria.

Watch for process toil, like reports nobody acts on, and for relay on-call, where the page should go straight to the team that can act. What doesn't work: declaring toil an emergency, making everyone fill in spreadsheets (toil on top of toil), declaring bug bankruptcy, naming a "toil expert", outsourcing toil to a team that isn't on-call, and handing toil to new hires.

Toil is acceptable for mitigation (a few days of manual restarts while you investigate a deadlock), for learning (log every command in a recovery drill, then turn them into reusable logical operations) and for human judgement (a human kept the trigger on database recovery, which paid off when a monitoring glitch fired every alert at once).

It isn't acceptable to cover product gaps, to absorb work that grows with the system, or to stand in for safe tools when access is restricted.

The big picture

Testing stands in for a perfect model of the world, and production is the only fully accurate environment, which is why testing in production keeps growing. Know the types: unit, integration, system, smoke, performance, regression, resilience, production tests (canaries above all), fuzzing, property-based, mutation, static analysis and agentic. Infrastructure for testing means version control, reproducible central runs and hermetic environments.

ObservableYou can tell whether a change is live.

Configuration is as dangerous as code. The VOIS invariants: Versioned, Observable, Independent of code deploys, Safe with explicit safe defaults. Test config at three levels: syntax (cheap, and invaluable in an emergency), semantic and dynamic.

Watch for drift. Canaries send production traffic to a subset during an incubation period and run continuous statistical analysis; they are especially valuable for config. Even emergency rollouts need a fast tested path.

DiRT began as a yearly week-long exercise and became a continuous programme. It works top-down and bottom-up, it's framed as making systems better rather than compliance, and it's marketed internally. Service-level test dimensions: capacity and stability, data, infrastructure, dependencies (including running a dependency at its SLO to see if clients still meet theirs), failure handling, operations, change, business continuity, observability (the most fruitful) and security.

Testing measures how wrong your mental model of the system is.   • how traffic tags, failure domains and pooling change the sums

The big picture

Capacity planning means forecasting demand and providing supply, and it is really a risk-versus-cost trade-off much like an error budget. Ask first whether you need it at all: if autoscaling and a fault-tolerant design absorb a shortfall, spend the effort elsewhere.

OrganicSteady growth. Forecast it from trends.
Planned inorganicLaunches, Black Friday, the Olympics. Plan it with the launch.
Unplanned inorganicGoing viral. Handle it with buffers, autoscaling and demand shaping.

Otherwise, forecast demand as organic growth plus inorganic events (planned launches, unplanned shocks); express it through a driver metric such as DAU or QPS; quantify the uncertainty with scenarios and backtesting; and include capacity in postmortems. Track the ratio of infrastructure to product demand as a living model and alert when it drifts.

Supply depends on fungibility (location, latency, regulation), lead time and elasticity, and your cloud provider's trade-offs become your risks, which means reservations for peaks you can't miss and scarce accelerators.

Latency and availability needs belong to traffic, not to the service, so tag traffic with criticality (shed the least important first), mark latency-tolerant work, and provision so you can drain a failure domain. Build capacity isolation and failure domains, and remember transitive dependencies.

In overload, use crumple zones: shaping demand is cheaper than adding supply; move supply away from low-value work and hold central inventory.

Efficiency means utilisation, the cost of capacity and business value per dollar. Look first at high-cost and low-utilisation services. Pooling works because the peak of sums is below the sum of peaks: Google's Flex oversubscribes logical quota against physical capacity, scales composite services within limits, and reclaims stranded capacity with latency-tolerant work, though never all of it.

Case: Google Meet in 2020 saw regional growth of up to 400×, handled with adaptive long-running incident response, separate workstreams, pooled resources, control knobs (load shedding, lower video quality) and attention to burnout.

The iron triangle of capacity

Spare capacity buys reliability and costs money. Running hot saves money and costs latency under load. There is no free corner, which is why the chapter keeps repeating that the target is a business choice.

Making capacity fungible

Stranded capacity is capacity you own in a place or shape you can’t use. The chapter’s figures show two ways out. Work that tolerates delay can fill the troughs of local demand, which lowers the global total. And you can make workloads easier to move:

Architect for constraintsBreak a rigid application into services that scale on their own and don’t care which region they run in.
Shift work in timeBatch, training and backfills run when interactive demand is low.
Improve connectivityA better network lets demand in one place use supply in another.

On AWS: Spot and Batch for time-shifted work, Auto Scaling groups with several instance types so you aren’t tied to one, and multi-Region designs so demand can follow supply.

First decide if you need it

Provisioning for every edge case is like aiming for 100% availability. Small workloads should just overprovision, because compute is cheap compared with engineering time. Simple autoscaling rules (add two instances above 70% CPU for five minutes, remove one below 40% with a floor of five) cover most services.

Demand

Organic demand you forecast from trends (regression, Holt–Winters, ARIMA). Planned inorganic demand comes from launches, the Olympics and Black Friday. Unplanned inorganic demand (going viral, an unannounced celebrity livestream) you handle with buffers, autoscaling and demand shaping.

Also budget for performance regressions and DDoS. Forecast in percentiles (P50, P95, P99) across scenarios, backtest old forecasts, and check postmortems for capacity contributions. Track the ratio of infrastructure to business metrics (cores per 1,000 active users) and alert when it shifts, which catches regressions. Shorter provisioning lead times shrink how far ahead you must forecast.

Supply and the plan of record

Constraints turn the unconstrained forecast into a plan of record, and layering business metrics on top tells you what a shortfall costs. Supply varies in fungibility, lead time and elasticity. Shared cloud supply carries contention risk, because the same event that spikes your traffic can strain the provider, so pre-buy dedicated capacity for big events. Don't do that permanently, except in regulated, trading or large ML-training settings.

Requirements belong to traffic, not services

Provision redundantly for the strict subset of traffic and serve relaxed traffic from spare capacity. Tag requests through OTel context with criticality (cheap to discard under overload) and latency tolerance (route it to idle datacentres on the other side of the world).

Shedding assumes you have enough global capacity for critical and batch traffic combined, sized for the single global peak rather than the sum of regional peaks, so batch work rejected in one region retries in another. Physical partitioning (reserved instances for users, spot for batch) is simple but costly; sharing with criticality is efficient but brings noisy neighbours. Clients you don't control can cause a thundering herd as you recover.

Failure domains through a capacity lens

Typical cloud SLAs are 99.5% for a zone and 99.99% for multi-region services. Avoid single-zone deployments. Pre-provisioned spare capacity beats autoscaling when the whole internet is scaling at once. Know where your provider's control plane lives, because the failover API may sit in the zone you're fleeing.

With two zones, losing one doubles the load on the other. Run evictable work in reserved resilience capacity. Multicloud cuts correlated failure, but only if your cross-cloud orchestration doesn't become the weak link.

Crumple zones and efficiency

Define the degradation profile: how much slower, when users notice, and whether failure means throttling or days of manual restore. A cat-photo app and a grid controller are different universes. Shaping demand is cheaper than adding supply; measure shedding so the business can see what it gave up.

Utilisation is usage over capacity. Prioritise costly services, don't assume low utilisation means waste (DDoS defences sit idle until needed), question high-frequency low-value work (a query every minute versus once a day) and skip cheap services.

Peak of sums and Flex

In the book’s example, provisioning each service separately at its own peak needs 60 units; pooled at the combined peak they need 48. Google's Flex pools quota and saves about 5–20%. It gives each account a ceiling and an optional committed floor, oversubscribes, and keeps unused capacity committed for a while to avoid cold starts.

Reclaiming stranded capacity (Maps reroutes background navigation requests across the globe) must still provision for the combined peak, or low-priority work fails at peak.

Supply and demand curves converge, and because you can reshape both the bins and their contents, it stops being simple bin packing. "Minimum viable capacity" brings riskier early capacity online, and tolerant workloads get routed to it.

Users rarely forgive data failures. Start with the law and ethics: children's data, retention and deletion (soft and hard deletes, TTLs; Bigtable has a "fast" deletion SLO and a guaranteed "eventual" one), residency, purpose restrictions and cross-division sharing limits.

The hierarchy of data needs. Each layer rests on the one before.

Classify data as original or derived (derived data that's expensive to rebuild counts as original), identifiable or not, and flag high-value personal data, which you should avoid collecting if you can. The hierarchy of data needs: integrity, then security, then availability, then efficiency.

Integrity comes from redundancy, checksums at every layer including RPCs (the case where a bad network cable corrupted replicas), append-only logs and MVCC, and application-level reconciliation.

Map failure scenarios to defences. Operator error or malice: backups, and no single person with unilateral access. Bad application writes: PITR or snapshots, transaction logs, reconciliation. Replication does not help with either, because the corruption or the delete replicates too.

A storage-system bug: back up into a different system. Hardware loss: erasure coding or replication. Region loss: cross-region copies. Objectives: RPO (how stale restored data may be, which sets backup frequency), RTO (Restore Time Objective; snapshots are fast but share fate with the source) and RWO (how far back you can restore, limited by deletion law and cost).

Replication involves single or multi-leader, sync or async with a lag bound, guaranteed or best-effort delivery, and planning for losing a leader. Snapshots are logical and share fate; backups are physical copies. Follow 3-2-1. Writers to live data get no admin rights over backups.

Weigh full, incremental and differential backups, and prefer simple over clever when restore reliability matters. Test restores of every type. Plan backups in five stages (discover and classify, set SLOs, choose architecture, design validation, get sign-off), cover new data sources automatically, and write a data recovery policy before you need it.

Why data failures are different

Lost data stays lost without a working backup, and exfiltrated data can't be pulled back. On 9/11, Aon's planning had it operating again within hours with no data lost. The chapter's authors run Bigtable: 10 exabytes and 7 billion queries per second.

Legal and ethical first

Check the law in every place where data originates, is stored and is processed.

  • Minors: COPPA needs verifiable adult consent under 13, and no behavioural profiles.
  • Retention and deletion: backups expiring counts as part of deletion, which caps backup retention. Support soft and hard deletes, and TTLs you can clear or disable. GDPR fines reach €20M or 4% of global revenue.
  • Bigtable offers a fast deletion SLO and an eventual one that it honours even when underprovisioned, by putting deletion ahead of user traffic.
  • Residency: Google Cloud's "hurricane policy" means regionally restricted data is never moved, even to save it, because those customers prefer data loss to breaking the law.
  • Purpose limits: under the DMA, music preferences aren't used for ads without consent, and HIPAA restricts patient data.
  • Ethics: aim to be "lawful good", not merely lawful.

Classification

Original data needs more care than derived data, but derived data that's expensive to rebuild (like the Search index) gets treated as original. Hashing or stripping identifiers doesn't anonymise data; it has to be generalised across many subjects. Avoid collecting high-value personal data (keys, government IDs, sensitive attributes) unless you truly need it.

Hierarchy of data needs

Integrity, then security, then availability, then efficiency, captured in the storage team's motto that you, and only you, can always read your data.

  • Integrity ("what you read is what you wrote") needs redundancy across disks, servers, racks and regions; DB constraints; checksums at the application, every storage layer and the RPC layer; append-only logs and MVCC.
  • The GFS case: one read in three failed application checksums. Swapping RAM, CPUs and switches didn't help; a faulty Cat5 cable was corrupting data in a way that passed the TCP, RPC and GFS checksums. Bigtable now sees about one minor silent-corruption event a year.
  • Reconciliation versus repair: reconciliation handles expected inconsistency by design; repair fixes unexpected inconsistency and signals a bug. Log repairs with debugging info and alert on a threshold, which may be one.
  • Security: exceptional access should be narrow (one account, limited time) and logged to a system the accessor doesn't control. Use least privilege, customer-managed keys and multi-party approval. Never test on production data; Google's "crash" instances are wiped regularly, and sandboxes are named nothing like production.
  • Availability: how often data is read is a poor proxy for how much its unavailability hurts.
  • Efficiency: cross-region "RAID" (half the data in A, half in B, parity in C) has 50% overhead instead of 100%, cutting cost by 25%.

Failure scenarios and defences

  • Operator error or malice (agents included): snapshots, backups, and no unilateral human access.
  • Bad application writes: snapshots, transaction logs for replay, invariant checks and reconciliation. Replication doesn't help.
  • Storage-system bugs: RocksDB research found roughly one corruption per 3 months per 100 PB, with 40% already copied to replicas. Use backups or dual writes to separate systems.
  • Hardware: change the encoding (RAID5 to RAID6), replicate, back up. Snapshots won't help.
  • Region loss: off-region backups and replication.

RPO, RTO, RWO

Set the objectives first and let them drive the design, not the other way round. RPO sets backup frequency, which limits how long each backup can take, which can force sharding and application changes. RTO is cumulative: detection, plus action, plus restore.

RPORecovery point: how much recent data you can afford to lose.
RTORestore time: how long until it’s back. The clock starts at the incident, so it includes time to detect, time to act and time to restore.
RWORestore window: how far back in time you can go.

It also tells you how much spare bandwidth a full restore needs. Partial restores can be slow: tape is sequential, a SQL backup restores whole tables, and tar archives must be read in full. RWO example: daily backups kept for two days can't recover corruption from three days ago, and with 14 days you can still lose up to 24 hours if the corruption landed at 23:50.

Replication

Replication alone isn't recovery: corruption propagates, and so do deletes. If async replication is five minutes behind, failing over means losing those five minutes. Three replicas in separate regions is the norm. Single-leader setups give ordering; multi-leader setups need conflict resolution, and last-write-wins may be wrong.

Async needs an upper bound and per-shard lag telemetry, and best-effort replication leads to divergent replicas. On losing a follower, wait or replace it; on losing a leader, promote one and bound the lost writes. Watch for split-brain.

Backups

  • Snapshots are copy-on-write metadata: cheap and quick, ideal before a risky change, but they share fate with live data.
  • Backups are separate physical copies. Follow 3-2-1, and anything that can write live data must have no admin rights over backups.
  • PITR is a backup plus a write log.
  • Full, incremental or differential: full backups are simple and independent but slow. Incrementals are cheapest, but one bad link breaks the chain. Differentials need only the full plus one diff, though each grows until the next full.
  • Smart vs simple: a raw file copy is smart but version-locked and fragile; a SQL export is simple and portable but spikes load.

The big picture

Resilience is keeping adequate service quality during failures, including ones nobody anticipated, or degrading clearly when you can't. Handle high-probability failures cheaply, prioritise by severity, and decide deliberately about rare but high-impact ones (some aren't worth the cost).

Responses can be manual, automated or architectural, and architectural is best because it removes the need to react at all.

Google's principles cover risk management, change management, dependency management, failure domains, overload management, input validation, data resilience, qualification and emergency mode, and they're enforced through common platforms, with exceptions sought explicitly and remaining risks escalated.

Change management: small, progressive changes across failure domains, including data and config; one change at a time; supervision; and safe mitigations: drain, roll back, upsize, degrade, block (a query of death). Mitigations obey the same rules and must be tested.

Set risk-tolerance levels from normal to medium, high and highest (only break-fixes and critical security). Use defence in depth: feature flags, emergency stops and independent health monitors that halt rollouts.

Dependencies: never depend on something less reliable than you need; document mechanism, criticality (optional or required) and when it's needed (build, startup, runtime); prove optional dependencies are optional; protect your dependents; plan fallbacks.

Failure domains: geographic, functional (partitioning or user sharding), infrastructure (racks, networks) and operational (the scope of operators' and automation's credentials). Align deployments to them, look for hidden shared dependencies, and keep a pristine known-good copy of global state in at least one domain.

To recover: isolate, repair, reassemble, and reach into other domains only with checks and balances. Overload: cascades come from long queues, missed deadlines that trigger retries, restarts and cold caches. On the server side, shed load early, by criticality, with an explicit retry signal, and degrade gracefully.

On the client side, use exponential backoff with jitter, adaptive throttling, retry budgets per request and per service, and per-user quotas. In an emergency, block excess sources, add capacity, stop the automation that restarts "unhealthy" servers, restart in a controlled way, and plan for a cold start.

Input validation: reject empty, partial or corrupt updates and huge diffs, keep serving the last good state, allowlist what "good" looks like, and alert. Data resilience: deglobalise risky operations, delete in stages with grace periods, keep independent fallback backups of metadata and keys, and plan fast recovery.

Qualification: keep tests fresh, realistic and mandatory, shift them left, and prove resilience in realistic environments. Emergency mode: incident tooling must keep working when core services are down.

Reliability vs resilience

Reliability means staying within designed norms. Resilience means detecting and handling active failures, novel ones included, with prepared responses. Five nines is a practical ceiling for many systems, but the remaining tail can still burn years of budget, so look for cheap partial mitigations.

Likelihood times impact breaks down for rare catastrophes. For likely failures, use cheap default-on mitigations and test with all optional dependencies disabled. For rare ones, first write down how you'd handle it today, then fully address a few rather than half-addressing many.

Prioritise what has happened to you, then what has happened to others, then realistic risks, then known unknowns.

Enforce through technology

A written policy alone overloads people; platform defaults and programmatic checks compound over time. A complex plan might work, but resilience has to work every time. Teams see risk vertically, through their own stack; leadership sees it horizontally, asking every team the same risk questions.

Change management details

  • Avoid both too many components in one update and several updates to one component.
  • Global data needs versioning, and its clients must fail safe on bad data.
  • Roll binaries and data separately: one zone, then two, then more. Never change interacting components (a server and its clients or schema) in parallel.
  • Google's Canary Analysis Service checks each replica's health; for the most critical systems, include the health of dependents too.
  • Rollback isn't guaranteed, so be ready to roll forward.
  • Feature flags have fewer dependencies than a binary rollback.
  • Give people an inherently safe emergency stop, and the psychological safety to press it.
  • Alert when more outages than usual are being declared, because the cause may be outside your system.

Dependencies

Lock down which dependencies are allowed, at build, config, startup and runtime. Production must never depend on non-production. Optional dependencies fail open; if you can't prove a dependency is optional, treat it as required. When defending dependents, restore critical ones first, start risky changes where dependents matter least, and protect your fallbacks.

A fallback can be an earlier version (cheap, but shares fate) or a distinct low-dependency implementation (expensive, but shares little).

Failure domains and alignment

A failure domain is only real if everything it needs is local. Alignment matters: two regions with two zones each lose 25% of capacity per zone, while one zone per region without anti-affinity loses 50%. Scale by adding zones rather than growing them, or a zone becomes too large to fail, since draining it would cause a thundering herd.

Recovery means isolating, repairing, reassembling and reaching the domain, keeping security patches current while parts are in repair, and keeping global recovery powers in safe places. Protect global state with a pristine domain: rollouts delayed by weeks, no emergency pushes, used only to recover others (it's not a warm standby).

Overload and input

The goal: a system sized for X that receives 10X should still serve about X and recover on its own. Degrade to roughly 80% quality for 20% of the compute. Load-test until goodput plateaus. Retry at one layer only, using standard client libraries.

Reuse per-user quotas for shedding and retry budgets: servers keep a per-request budget, and clients a per-service one. Treat config and code as control inputs. Reject empty updates (an empty ACL locks everyone out), use expected-size fields, keep the previous version in memory to swap back, and alert on bad input.

Data resilience and qualification

Risks include losing metadata, losing encryption keys (restores need them) and lower storage layers dropping replicas without understanding the data. Deglobalise storage rollouts, pause mutating operations when redundancy is short, block accidental TTLs on critical data, keep independent metadata backups in a different DBMS, and preserve keys.

Qualify through pretend disaster role-play (D&D meets IMAG), then run safer variants for real. Isolating a test environment from DNS and load balancers exposed local cyclic dependencies. One drill revealed too little quota to restore all user data.

Incident modes and response journeys

  • Normal: rich tools for everyone.
  • Emergency: low-dependency fallbacks with the same interfaces, usable by most responders with minimal training. Invest here first, because it's more likely than disaster.
  • Disaster: zero-dependency CLIs and direct network access, for small trained teams.

Tools need to keep their security posture in every mode, and errors should point people to the fallback. Response user journeys are comms, IMAG, dashboards, reaching people, docs, privileged access (including multi-party approval), debugging and fixing.

Train people to know these exist, then practise them in order: core (reach, comms, privileged access, docs, IMAG), then common (dashboards, debugging, fixing), then bespoke. A common regression is a fallback quietly acquiring a dependency on the system it's meant to replace.

What a platform is

A platform is the abstraction over your infrastructure plus the culture around it. A good one makes the reliable way the default. A horizontal SRE team (advisory, shared tools, standards) does mostly platform engineering.

Google moved from "bring me requirements, not solutions" to requirements plus curated solutions: a vetted set of tools, not one monolith, because the old approach led to duplication and made it hard for SREs to move between teams. Curation done well makes the right way the easy way.

Policy as a style guide for production

Enforce it with compliance checkers, the way a linter enforces a style guide: in the editor, at commit or at runtime, minding how much noise each adds. Ask what goal a rule serves before adding it. Use warnings unless you're certain no exceptions exist, and even errors need a break-glass path.

Models to cite

  • Cynefin: the domains are clear, complicated, complex, chaotic and, in the middle, confusion. A platform moves accidental complexity towards clear, making the routine 90% easy so experts can focus on the hard 10%.
  • Systems thinking: system dynamics, Meadows' leverage points, and Ashby's law of requisite variety (a controller needs at least as many states as the system it controls).
  • Direct versus indirect control: pulling a weed versus improving the soil.
  • Legibility (James C. Scott): make systems readable by imposing order.

Use strong defaults (JVM tuning), small menus (regional, continental or global databases) and remove options whose downsides dominate. More from the chapter

If generating code becomes nearly free, what becomes expensive? The development lifecycle phases stay the same but shrink, and a two-hour build phase leaves no time for the small refinements a two-week one allowed. Fast feedback helps functional requirements, not reliability or security.

AI-written code is about as reliable as before (there's evidence it may be less), and models learned from far more code that merely works than code that's reliable in production. Conway's law weakens when one person with one agent can take a spec to production.

Non-functional trade-offs, like whether to prioritise paid over free traffic, are social, and AI may recommend, decide or design them away.

The predictions:

  • The rate of change will outrun human timescales, cherry-picking fixes stops working, and roll-forward may become primary, the reverse of the usual SRE preference.
  • Ten times the delivery likely means about ten times the incidents. Automation will take the easy cases, so humans see the hard ones at the rate they used to see easy ones (and how do you learn on those?).
  • Strategic incoherence: agents handle low-context tasks well, but coherence degrades as features pile up, duplication bloats the context window, and agents become tireless generators of technical debt. Specification-driven development counters this.
  • Versioning may need to work per request; "what's actually running?" becomes hard to answer; staging may become impossible.
  • Debugging context has to be built on the fly with lossy tools; there may be disposable production environments, replaced when recovery is exhausted; causal chains get longer (more suspects in the murder mystery).
  • Cheap response could starve prevention of funding, and skills erode while tools are weakest in the tail where SREs work.

The bright spots are cheaper observability (with more duplicated metrics) and tractable formal methods. Humans remain the understanders of last resort, with comprehension shifting from when code is written to when it fails, and the SRE role starts to look like a staff engineer's.

The stack and engagement

Google's first ML SRE team started in 2009 for ad click prediction. Four SLO pillars

Availability covers hardware, the training control plane and attestation. Throughput is measured as ML goodput, the share of allocated time doing useful work. Latency covers time to first token, scheduling latency and data staleness.

Quality covers golden datasets, prediction bias and tool-call success rates. For online learning, put SLOs on upstream data producers, and have degraded inputs pause training before the model is corrupted. Evaluate in tiers: lineage checks (halt on freshness or volume drops), cheap statistical drift checks, then sampled model-as-judge scoring.

An eval score is an SLI, and its pass threshold is the SLO. For agents, track tool-use success, valid JSON and divergence from the task, since an early hallucination cascades. Security SLOs cover evidence freshness and attestation availability, because for Private AI Compute an attestation failure is an outage.

Reliability is a spectrum

Responses range from server errors, to the model giving up or answering poorly, to good answers. Instrument intentional degradation (smaller-model fallbacks) and safety-filter hits. Capture explicit feedback (downvotes) and implicit feedback (prompt rewrites, abandonment). Load tests need quality thresholds. Request costs vary wildly, so uniform-cost assumptions and simple thresholds break.

Qualification

Hyrum's law hits harder because researchers depend on undocumented behaviour. Qualify accelerators continuously from late engineering validation onwards, testing performance, correctness and relative quality across a workload × architecture × topology matrix prioritised by production usage.

Hardware only becomes SLO-eligible after a steady stream of qualification data (pre-release and obsolete hardware aren't eligible). Gate new models on structure, op compatibility and resource estimates. Weights, preambles and prompt templates are all change surfaces to canary.

Performance gates watch duty cycle and HBM; quality gates use golden sets, human ratings and A/B comparison. Agent workflows need scenario tests for task success, robustness and the absence of destructive actions.

The loss framework

Utilisation is measured as duty cycle (for example, P95 occupancy over seven days). Starting from 100%, six losses explain the gap:

  1. Max utilisation: real-time serving may queue at around 50%, batch at around 90%. Find the real limit with a redline canary.
  2. Unrealised demand: fill launch buffers with preemptible work, pool capacity, grow in steps.
  3. N+1 redundancy: use smaller failure domains, and scope redundancy to the critical classes.
  4. Disaggregation imbalance: tune the prefill-to-decode server ratio.
  5. Load balancing: request costs are uneven and KV-cache locality pulls against even spread. Use least-loaded or peak-EWMA routing, plus cost-aware routing by input tokens.
  6. Fragmentation: a large model has a minimum footprint (say 100 chips), so consolidate models, prefer prompting over fine-tuning, and serve multiple tenants.

The safety loop for an agent with production access

  • The agent sends its reason with the action, so a reviewer or an audit can check the thinking, not only the command.

Framing

Probabilistic tools at production scale bring probabilistic failure at scale: a 1% error rate spread across all of production is a catastrophe. Organise around three capabilities. Experimentation treats results as provisional, because what fails today may work in months.

Productionisation adds guardrails and evals and keeps validating, because a more capable model may take more action than you expect. Enablement: the hardest part is rebuilding senior engineers' intuition. The strategy follows the hierarchy of production health. The book expects more SRE work rather than less (the Jevons paradox).

Safety architecture

Production has no safe sandbox: a coding agent's worst case is a failed build, while a production agent's is draining a healthy cluster. The three pillars:

  • Transparency: the agent shows its reasoning, signals and confidence.
  • Contextual awareness: freezes, error budgets and active incidents all count.
  • Progressive authorisation: read-only, then proposing, then autonomous for low-risk actions.

Start with least privilege and dry-run audits. If the error budget is exhausted, downgrade the agent to advisory. Give agents SLOs too: proposal latency, a helpfulness SLO (say 95% of suggestions accepted) and a safety budget that pauses autonomy when spent.

Keep a break-glass block on agent access. Reversible operations: act only if you know the undo, and validate over a context-aware window that includes warm-up. Treat act, validate and roll back as one transaction. Agents force the strict reversibility the industry usually skips by fixing forward.

The foundations are real-time retrieval over topology and incident history, MCP tools, agent-to-agent protocols and agent orchestration.

Coupling: decide what depends on what

You will learn
  • Explicit and implicit coupling, tight and loose, and how cohesion relates
  • Four reasons to couple on purpose and four costs of doing it
  • Five patterns that loosen coupling and four antipatterns that tighten it by accident

The big picture

Coupling is how much the parts of a system depend on each other. It is usually talked about as a defect. This chapter’s position is more useful: some coupling is unavoidable, some is valuable, and the real failure is coupling nobody chose.

Two distinctions set up everything else.

MeaningExample
ExplicitOne part calls another directlyA service makes an RPC to another service
ImplicitNo direct interaction, but one part assumes the other exists or behaves a certain wayA producer writes to a queue. It has never heard of the consumer, and a change to the message format still breaks it
TightParts know each other’s internalsA service reads another team’s database tables
LooseParts know only each other’s interfacesA service calls a versioned API

Cohesion is the mirror image: how closely related the things inside one module are. A module with low cohesion does many unrelated jobs, so either it depends on many things or many things depend on it. Low cohesion and high coupling tend to arrive together.

When coupling pays

EfficiencyOne safe path for everyone. Google’s tooling is coupled to its issue tracker, so every change has a tracked reason and bugs close themselves when the fix is fully rolled out.
PerformanceFewer layers and hops. Spanner can interleave child rows with their parent. Maths lives in libraries, not services, because an RPC would cost more than the sum.
AtomicityAll-or-nothing changes. Deleting a cloud project must also delete its VMs and disks, or resources leak.
TraceabilityFollow data end to end. Google’s tracing works everywhere because every service uses the same RPC layer, which gives one place to instrument.

Google has a name for what you give up: retargetability, the ability to reuse a part of the system for a different purpose. The trade is engineering efficiency against retargetability.

One warning on performance. Couple for speed only after other options are exhausted. Tight coupling can block later optimisation, because speeding up one module may break a timing assumption in another.

When coupling costs

ReuseCoupled parts can’t be used alone. And reuse itself creates coupling: when the tiny left-pad package was unpublished in 2016, thousands of builds broke.
ExtensibilityA change in one place has side effects elsewhere, and isolated tests are hard to write.
RobustnessHyrum’s law: with enough users, every observable behaviour is depended on by someone. Failures cascade.
Freedom of choiceLock-in. Teams can’t adopt something better without engineering work, and can’t self-serve.

Finding the balance

No platform can be the best on every axis. A tool that does one job will eventually beat the platform at that job, and users will want to leave for it. Leaving needs loose coupling. Meeting expectations across several axes needs tight coupling. The chapter’s answer is to look for the design that gets the most benefit from the least coupling, which is Pareto efficiency: a point where you can’t improve one dimension without giving up another.

How do you choose the point? Ask what your users value most. If they need raw performance, couple. If they need to extend and customise, don’t.

Google’s own tradeSource control, code review, build and deployment are tightly coupled at Google. That lets them mark one change as bad so it can never ship, even by accident. When Log4Shell appeared, a central profiler wired into the build and scheduling systems showed quickly that exposure was small. The price was that every team had to use the same tools, whatever they were missing.
Going deeper types, patterns, antipatterns and how to find hidden coupling

Five kinds of coupling

The chapter borrows Kruchten’s “4+1” architectural views and asks, for each one, how two components can be tied together.

ViewCoupled when…How restrictive

The scenario examples are memorable. A shark bit one of Google’s undersea cables. When many people left Twitter in 2022, they overwhelmed Mastodon’s servers. Neither pair of systems shared a line of code.

Patterns that loosen coupling

Antipatterns that tighten it by accident

Shared databaseSeveral services read and write the same tables. The schema becomes the interface, so every schema change needs a coordinated release. Fix: a store per service behind an API, and let the platform provide backup and replication.
Distributed monolithServices look separate and aren’t. Signs: releases must be orchestrated in order, they must sit in the same rack, or one failing takes all down. The costs of microservices with the constraints of a monolith.
Megamorphic artifactA config file or object that grew into a junk drawer. Nobody knows who reads which part, so every edit is risky.
Control couplingA flag lets the caller steer the callee’s internal behaviour. The caller now knows how the callee works. Fix: split into separate operations.

Data warehouses and lakes are not the shared-database antipattern. Their consumers expect schemas to change and use registries and ETL tools to cope.

The megamorphic file at Compute EngineOne large configuration file started as infrastructure as code. Because it was easy to extend, it collected per-customer preferences and behaviour flags. Almost every service read a piece of it and none needed all of it. Small edits began causing rollbacks and minor incidents. The team split it into smaller, cohesive files, audited every consumer, and removed access nobody needed. Dependencies became visible and the blast radius of a bad edit shrank.

The odd one: workflow engines

A workflow engine runs a process as a state machine and retries each step until the whole thing finishes. That decouples steps nicely, provided every step is idempotent and has no side effects beyond its output. Data pipelines usually qualify. Elsewhere, three problems appear.

  • Stuck flows. A step that can never succeed is retried forever. Someone must detect it and decide whether to roll back or leave things half done.
  • Orphaned state. Early steps wrote data that the failed workflow never used. You need compensating actions (the saga pattern) or a janitor job, and both are coupled to the business logic of the steps.
  • Version skew. A step is redeployed while workflows are in flight, and later steps depended on its old behaviour.

Layers: parts of a platform change at different speeds

You will learn
  • What the OSI model, shearing layers and pace layers each teach about platforms
  • The six-layer framework for platform engineering, from fastest to slowest
  • How the layers push on each other, told through one internal tool

The big picture

Abstractions give you reusable components. Coupling describes how they connect. Layers add time: which parts of a platform change quickly, which change slowly, and how they influence each other.

That matters because a platform team is always balancing two speeds. Product teams want to ship this week. Governance, security and compliance move on a scale of quarters and years. A layered view tells you which speed belongs where, so the fast parts can move without shaking the slow ones.

Three earlier ideas about layers

ModelFromWhat it gives platform engineers
OSI modelZimmermann, 1980Three benefits of strict layering: each layer adds value to those below, the problem is divided into manageable pieces, and a layer can change inside as long as it offers the same service upward. It is prescriptive, and real systems bend it.
Shearing layersFrank Duffy; Stewart Brand, 1994A building is six layers that age at different rates: site, structure, skin, services, space plan, stuff. The site is forever and the furniture moves weekly. It is descriptive, which suits platforms, since they mirror their organisation and resist forced standards.
Pace layersStewart Brand, 1999Civilisation as six layers by speed: fashion, commerce, infrastructure, governance, culture, nature. Health comes from the tension between them.

Brand’s summary of how fast and slow layers relate is the line to remember. The fast layers learn and propose. The slow layers remember and decide. Shocks are absorbed by the fast layers at the top, and stability comes from each layer respecting the pace of its neighbours.

Six layers of platform engineering

LayerWhat lives herePace
1 · Prototypes and experimentsBuilding to learn. Most of it is thrown away, and the lessons decide what moves down. Rust experiments at Google eventually shaped company-wide directives.Days to weeks
2 · ProductsThe marketplace. Proven ideas become features with requirements and stability. Product teams think about what, not how.Months
3 · InfrastructureThe paved road: runtimes, server frameworks, networking, CI/CD. Convergence here saves each team tens or hundreds of person-years, and every improvement reaches everyone.Quarters or longer
4 · Technical directivesThe law of the engineering organisation: binding cross-company decisions, such as safety expectations for AI agents or converging on a set of server frameworks.Quarters to years
5 · Business cultureWhy the company does what it does. Durable system health over short-term gain; protecting users by default.Years
6 · Regulatory and legalThe outside world. Everything above must eventually comply. A change here cascades upward through culture, directives and infrastructure.Slow, then sudden

One tool, six layers

The chapter follows an internal screenshot tool called SnipIt through every layer. It is a good story to retell because it shows the layers pushing on each other in both directions.

  1. PrototypeStarted in 2009 as a 20% project. Nobody would have funded a screenshot tool if asked.
  2. ProductFor more than 15 years a tiny team, often one person part-time, kept it running. By 2024 it was the third most used internal application at Google.
  3. InfrastructureIt reached 8 billion screenshots with no major rework, because it ran on App Engine. The leverage was so complete it was invisible.
  4. DirectiveSecurity standards moved on, and the tool needed finer-grained access controls. The first decision was to shut it down.
  5. CultureEmployees pushed back hard: testimonials, petitions, memes. Shutting down something this useful was called “ungoogley”.
  6. LegalThe obligation to protect internal data could not be argued with. So the only remaining answer was to invest and bring the tool up to standard.

Read it as two forces. A fast layer proposed something and it worked. A slow layer said no. A slower layer overruled that no, and the slowest layer set the terms of the yes.

Going deeper how to use the model

Using the layers in practice

  • Place every decision on a layer before arguing about it. A choice of library is layer 1 or 2 and should be cheap to reverse. A choice of deployment framework is layer 3. A mandate is layer 4 and needs an owner and a reason.
  • Let ideas move down only when proven. The top layers are where you learn cheaply. Promote what works; don’t standardise an experiment.
  • Don’t make slow layers move at the speed of fast ones. Rewriting your deployment platform every quarter to follow a trend is how platforms lose trust.
  • Expect shocks from the bottom. When regulation changes, plan the cascade: what the policy becomes, which directive it turns into, which platform component enforces it.

The AI chapter later uses the same picture: AI applications are the fast layer, and the platform is the slow layer that absorbs shocks and gradually takes on whatever has proven itself.

Connection to the SRE trackChapter 3 of the SRE book describes compliance being written as policy-as-code and checked before deployment. In this model that is a layer 6 requirement, turned into a layer 4 directive, implemented at layer 3.
Say it in an interview

I think about a platform as layers that change at different speeds. Experiments and products at the top change in days or months. Infrastructure, technical directives, culture and regulation below them change over quarters and years. The fast layers are where we learn; the slow layers are where we keep what we learned. Most platform arguments get easier once you agree which layer a decision belongs to.

On AWSA workable mapping: sandbox accounts for experiments (layer 1), product accounts and services (layer 2), the shared platform of landing zone, EKS or ECS base, pipelines and golden-path templates (layer 3), Service Control Policies, Config conformance packs and tagging standards as technical directives (layer 4), and compliance frameworks tracked in Audit Manager and Security Hub standards at the bottom (layer 6). Use AWS Organizations to give the layers different rates of change: sandbox OUs with loose guardrails, production OUs with strict ones.

Interview practice

Answer aloud for two minutes before you open the model answer.

A team wants to use a new language that the platform doesn’t support. How do you decide?

Show a model answer

I’d start by asking which layer the request sits on. Trying a language in a prototype is a top-layer activity. It should be cheap and allowed, in a sandbox, without platform guarantees, and the team should know they own everything the platform would normally give them.

Putting it into a product that pages someone is a different request. Now it touches the infrastructure layer: build, dependency management, security scanning, observability and deployment all need to work for that language. That is a real cost, paid by the platform team for years.

So I’d set a path. Experiment freely. If the experiment shows a clear advantage for a class of workloads, write that up, and decide at the directive level whether to add the language to the paved road. Google’s Rust adoption followed that route: exploration first, then a company-wide direction, then investment in tooling.

What I’d avoid is the middle state, where a production service runs on a stack nobody supports. That gives the team the freedom of a low-integration ecosystem while they still expect the guarantees of a high-integration one.

Shared fate and shared responsibility

You will learn
  • What shared fate means and why it needs shared responsibility
  • The economic trade: architectural choice for platform liability
  • Resources, rights and responsibilities: the three Rs of the social contract
  • Why guardrails matter even more when AI agents write the code

The big picture

In a mature engineering ecosystem, components are connected on purpose. One team’s library update, security patch or shortcut reaches everyone who builds on it. The book calls this shared fate. It is what makes fleet-wide improvements possible, and it is also what makes fleet-wide failures possible.

Shared fate without shared responsibility ends in a tragedy of the commons. Each team does what is locally sensible, such as adding a risky dependency or ignoring a deprecation notice, and the common ground everyone depends on gets worse.

The economics: choice for liability

The whole chapter rests on one equation. The more an ecosystem limits a developer’s architectural choices, the more liability it can take on for that developer.

Type 4 · highly integratedType 2 or 3 · loosely integrated
You getLeverage. The platform owns security, reliability, efficiency and complianceFlexibility and a fast start. Any language, library or toolchain
You payConformity. Standard build system, languages and tools. The head of every library must always build and pass testsThe operational load comes back to you. You are your own security engineer and compliance officer
Good forMost production software at scaleAcquisitions, special projects, early experiments

Two of Google’s numbers show the dividend. A central improvement to the C++ memory allocator reached every team at once and saved a significant share of fleet CPU, with no team changing a line. And safe-by-design query APIs produced zero SQL injection in more than a decade in one ecosystem. Neither result came from training thousands of engineers.

One example across the types: third-party packages

Type 3Handles ingestion and gives you scan dataChoosing versions and deciding how to mitigate each vulnerability
Type 4Ingests, scans for malware, enforces freshness and licences, blocks bad packagesAlmost nothing, and almost no choice

The chapter separates a resource from a guardrail with this example. A registry is a resource: it helps, and the easiest thing to do with it is ignore updates. A managed dependency system is a guardrail: the safe package is the only one you can get.

Governance: from policing to stewardship

Central enforcement works in a uniform ecosystem, where it can be automated until developers no longer notice it. It does not work across mixed environments, where a central team can’t enforce what it can’t see.

There the platform’s job changes to stewardship. It provides tools, data and guidance, including coverage metrics that let each team lead see how well they meet their own obligations. Accountability for accepting risk sits with the local team. The shift is from enforcement to enablement, and it keeps the central team from becoming a bottleneck.

Guardrails: the path of least resistance

You can’t run a large system on willpower. The safest action has to be the easiest one.

Type 2Find defects after the commit: static analysis, fuzzing. The developer must triage and fix
→
Type 4Assurance by design: safe-by-default frameworks, and raw risky APIs locked away from everyone except reviewed code

The chapter calls this the hierarchy of assurance. Enforcement is mechanical, through build visibility rules, and never by convention alone.

A useful phrase from the chapter: friction is a directive. People do what is easy. Remove friction from safe behaviour and add it to risky behaviour, and the system steers itself.

Uniformity also pays in maintenance. With one compiler and build chain, the platform team can make a large-scale change across thousands of projects quickly, for example to respond to a new security threat. In a mixed environment every team patches by hand.

Going deeper the social contract, its edges, and AI agents

The three Rs

ResourcesWhat the ecosystem gives the developer: compute, storage, safe APIs, managed dependencies.
RightsWhat the developer is allowed. In a loose ecosystem, unlimited choice. In an integrated one, that right is given up in exchange for the right to assured safety.
ResponsibilitiesWhat the developer puts in to keep the ecosystem healthy.

The contract is most often broken in one specific way: a team wants the rights of a loose ecosystem and the resources of an integrated one. Free choice of stack, and automatic security. Those two can’t be had together, and saying so explicitly is most of the work.

Where the contract strains

The friction shows at the edges. An acquired company arrives with a very different ecosystem. A team spinning out a special project tries to take the big machinery with it and finds it can’t run it alone. The chapter asks for permeability: moving between ecosystems is a renegotiation of the contract, and it goes better when framed as a trade the team is making, not a mandate imposed on them.

Why this matters more with AI agents

An agent writes code faster than people can review it. In a loose ecosystem, where safety depends on human vigilance and manual glue, that speed multiplies mistakes. In a tightly integrated one, the platform’s constraints act as a containment field: an agent can’t commit code that breaks a safety rule, because that code doesn’t build.

Agents also do better when there is less to get right. If the platform handles build flags, dependencies and safety properties, the agent only has to express the business intent. The human’s role moves to being a guardian of intent: define the goal and the success criteria precisely, trust the fast generation, and verify deliberately.

Not everything belongs in the walled gardenThe chapter ends on a menu of deliberate trade-offs. Start experiments where friction is low. Let acquisitions integrate gradually. What matters is that each team knows which contract it has signed.

Learning loops

Incidents, audit findings and red-team results are raw material for shared learning. To use them you need two kinds of metrics: team-level delivery metrics in the DORA style, and ecosystem-level ones such as toolchain effectiveness and governance coverage.

Say it in an interview

Shared fate means one team’s choices land on everyone, so it has to come with shared responsibility. I frame it as a trade: the more a platform limits your choices, the more liability it can carry for you. I make that explicit with three Rs: what resources the platform gives, what rights the team has, and what responsibilities they keep. The failure I watch for is a team wanting free choice of stack and automatic guarantees at the same time.

On AWSAWS publishes its own shared responsibility model, and your internal platform needs one too. A type 2 account gets raw services and a registry (ECR, CodeArtifact) and owns everything else. Publish a coverage dashboard so every team lead can see their own posture.

Efficiency: one optimisation, every team

You will learn
  • Why a common platform turns performance work into leverage
  • On by default, absorbed regressions and opt-outs
  • Application productivity metrics: telling “busier” from “slower”
  • Three lessons: take the long view, the platform is not fixed, build levers

The big picture

When every team runs on the same foundations, two things become possible. You can see where resources go across the whole fleet. And when you improve a shared component, everyone gets faster at once. Seeing leads to improving, and improving pays for more seeing. The chapter calls it a virtuous cycle.

Common infrastructureSame scheduler, RPC layer and libraries
→
Fleet-wide observabilityA profiler on every machine
→
Central optimisationFix the shared thing once
→
SavingsWhich fund more observability

Each new measurement rides on rails that already exist, so the next insight is cheaper than the last.

How Google got there

  • The scheduler hides placement. Borg decides which jobs share a machine. A CPU-heavy team and a memory-heavy team fit together, and spare capacity for failures is pooled across the data centre, not held by each team.
  • The same agents run on every machine. That made it possible to profile everything: Google-Wide Profiling gives each team a debugging tool and gives the company one aggregated picture.
  • The aggregate showed surprises. In 2015 the memory allocator accounted for about 8% of fleet usage, more than any single application. Nobody would have found that by looking at one service.
Profile-guided optimisation, made automaticCompilers can optimise better with a profile of real usage. Getting one used to mean each team building a load test, an instrumented binary and a second build step, so few did. Google used the fleet profiler to synthesise a profile for every team automatically, and adoption became a one-line change. In its first year this doubled the share of the fleet using the technique. The lesson: effort deters adoption more than doubt does.

Shifting optimisations down

On by default

The platform team prototypes an optimisation, canaries it on major workloads, evaluates it, and then turns it on for everyone. Teams pick it up at their next normal release and do nothing. This avoids the trap of building something and waiting for teams to discover it, and it keeps the decision with the people who understand the change.

Not every workload gets faster. The chapter is candid about how regressions are handled.

RegressionWhat they doWhy
Small, for a fewAbsorb itPer-application tuning adds options, and every option enlarges the set of states each future change must be tested against
Large, for a fewOffer an opt-out per application, and work to remove itAn opt-out is information: it shows which edge cases truly matter. One long-standing opt-out led to two further improvements
Large, for manyDon’t ship

The judgement behind this: an imperceptible slowdown or a little opt-out work for one team can be better than not shipping a major win for hundreds.

Spread the best local idea

A better hash table began as a collaboration between the core C++ library team and two engineers on the search index. The index team could have swapped their hottest tables and stopped. Instead the platform team rolled the new table out across the whole codebase with automated refactoring, and CPU and memory needs fell substantially.

A detail worth knowing: the designers made the table’s iteration order random on purpose. Earlier attempts to optimise hash tables had been blocked because code quietly relied on the order (Hyrum’s law again). Randomising it stopped that dependency from forming, which kept the door open for later improvements. Teams sometimes ask to switch it off. Saying yes would help them today and hurt everyone, including them, later.

Work further up the stack

A low-level copy routine can only be made so fast, because it must do the copy. Higher up, analysis of the code may show the copy was never needed. The fastest copy is the one you don’t make. Tools from linters to language models find these recurring patterns, and the central team sends ready-made changes to owners for review.

Shift left for performance too

  • Fleet telemetry raises alerts when a function, application or library regresses after a rollout.
  • Canaries pause for automated performance checks against the old version, not only reliability checks.
  • Benchmarks run periodically and on proposed changes.
  • Code-review tooling flags known slow patterns and proposes the fix.
  • Well-designed libraries make the efficient way the obvious way.
Going deeper measuring what matters

Connect efficiency to business value

Nobody buys servers in order to allocate memory. Low-level numbers are easy to measure and say little about value. What matters is how much useful work you get for what you buy.

The problem: a service’s CPU use went up 20%. Is that a regression, or is it handling more traffic? You can’t tell by looking.

Google’s answer is the application productivity metric (APM). Each service reports how much useful work it does, and the platform divides by resources used: videos processed per CPU-second, records per byte-second of memory, queries per accelerator-hour.

CPU usedUseful workWork per CPU-secondVerdict
+20%+20%FlatBusier. Nothing is wrong
+20%FlatDown 17%A regression. Find the change
Flat+15%Up 15%An optimisation landed
  • Defaults make it practical. RPC servers report requests; pipelines report records. Most services need no setup.
  • Outliers reveal bad metrics. A batch service should count items, not batches. A service whose work metric doesn’t track its resource use at all is probably misconfigured.
  • They catch what component metrics hide. Work on the allocator’s layout decisions made applications faster and made the allocator look more expensive in relative terms. Only a work-based metric showed the win. The team’s joke was that the allocator had become faster than light.

Not all work is equally valuable

Some resource use serves no user directly and is still necessary: monitoring probes, hedged requests, load tests, load balancing and shedding. Some serves nobody: a video still playing to an empty room, or a backend finishing a request whose caller has already given up. Deadline propagation stops the second kind. Knowing the criticality of each kind lets you shed the non-essential during a disruption and push background work off-peak, which means less idle capacity held in reserve.

Proxies and GoodhartMeasuring precisely costs something too. Use proxies that track the business goal closely, and remember Goodhart’s law: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure.

Security: if it isn’t secure, it shouldn’t compile

You will learn

  • Security as an emergent property, application archetypes and invariants
  • Safe Coding: risky operations, safe abstractions and the two ways to build one
  • Supply chain (SLSA, attestations), safe deployment and security observability

The big picture

The same kinds of vulnerability top the incident reports year after year: memory corruption, injection, broken access control. The industry’s list of the most dangerous weaknesses has barely changed in a long time, despite decades of secure-coding training.

This chapter’s diagnosis is that the problem is structural. We ask every developer to avoid every known mistake at every call site, forever, and we check afterwards with tools that can only find some of what went wrong. People make mistakes because the systems they work in make mistakes easy. So do AI coding tools, which now write more code than humans can review.

The platform answer: move security out of each developer’s head and into the things all code is built from, so whole classes of bug can’t be written.

What “platform” means hereMore than the cluster your code runs on. It is the whole stack a developer touches: the language and its type system, libraries and frameworks, the build system, deployment and runtime, and the review and release process. Application bugs are born in the layers nearest the developer, so that is where they can be prevented.

Why the usual approaches fall short

Three facts frame the problem.

  • Experts are outnumbered several hundred to one. The ratio is about the same at Google as at a small company. Hiring can’t fix it.
  • Software is programming integrated over time. Code that was secure at launch may not be after next week’s commit, so a point-in-time review proves little.
  • Testing can’t show absence. Even a tiny program has more possible states than any test suite can visit. A two-by-two grid of 64-bit numbers has about as many states as there are atoms in the observable universe. For ordinary bugs this is tolerable, because a bug nobody hits hardly matters. For security it isn’t, because an attacker goes looking for the one input you never tried.

That last point defines the goal. Assurance is confidence that a requirement holds for every behaviour of the program. Continuous assurance is keeping that confidence for every version you ship.

Find after commitStatic analysis, fuzzing, sanitizers, scanners, security review (increasingly by AI)Tools miss bugs or raise false alarms, usually both. Findings arrive after the author has moved on. Useful as defence in depth; can’t prove nothing remains

The chapter separates how fast you ship the first version from how fast you keep shipping. Shortcuts buy the first and cost the second. A platform pays the upfront cost once so applications inherit both. For a young product still finding its market, trading sustained speed for initial speed can be rational, if it is a conscious choice with a plan to pay it back.

Shift down: the platform as a security control

Security can’t be added afterwards. It emerges from how the system is designed, how the software is structured, and what the platform allows. That works in both directions.

If the platform enforces itApplications inherit the guarantee. In Rust, Java or Python, ordinary code can’t corrupt memory, and the exceptions are explicit and reviewable.
If the platform doesn’tApplications will have the defect. Any large C++ codebase contains memory-safety bugs, and decades of effort haven’t removed them after the fact.

Shifting down also compounds. Each class of bug the platform eliminates frees the security team from reviewing, advising and responding for that class. They spend the time eliminating the next one.

Three objectives

  1. Prevent common classes by construction. Design the platform so the bug can’t be introduced.
  2. Confine what is left. Keep security-critical code in a few well-reviewed platform components, not spread across every application.
  3. Keep the guarantee through change without an expert reviewing each commit.

Prevention never reaches zero defects, so containment stays essential: sandboxes, least-privilege identities and mediated access limit the damage of whatever slips through.

Application archetypes

Most applications are not architecturally unique. A stateless RPC service, a SQL-backed store, a browser frontend talking to an API, a mobile client: the same shapes recur, and the same threats come with each shape whether the app is a bank or a photo editor. The chapter calls a recurring whole-application shape an archetype.

An invariant is a property that holds on every execution path, including for malicious input. A platform can’t own your business logic, but it can own these, and they account for most of what goes wrong in practice.

A platform built for a whole archetype beats a set of separate controls, because some risks live between components. A browser frontend and an authenticated API can each be secured and still leave a gap: an attacker’s page can make the victim’s browser call a state-changing endpoint, unless cross-site defences cover the entire API surface. That depends on the boundary, which neither side owns alone.

Going deeper Safe Coding, the supply chain, deployment, observability and AI

Safe Coding

This is the heart of the chapter. Start with one definition.

A risky operation is safe only if some condition holds when it runs, and nothing in the language or API guarantees that it does. Reading element i of an array is safe only if i is in bounds. Running a SQL string is safe only if it was assembled from trusted parts. The condition is often established far away, in another module or another team’s code, so checking it means reasoning about the whole program.

A safe abstraction wraps the risky operation in a module with two properties.

  1. Its public interface puts no safety conditions on the caller. Any use that type-checks is safe.
  2. Its implementation takes full responsibility for the conditions of the risky operation inside.

The consequence is the important part: you can verify the abstraction by reading only its own code. A security expert reviews it once and it is safe for every caller, including applications not written yet.

Two ways to build one

TechniqueHow it worksExample
Runtime checkEstablish the condition immediately before the risky operationA vector type stores its length and checks the index on every access
Type invariantA purpose-built type whose existence is the evidence. If you hold a value of that type, the condition is trueA query type that can only be built from constants in the source code

The injection example

The chapter uses a photo app that looks up an album by ID, restricted to the signed-in owner. Here is the same idea in Python.

# Risky: structure and data travel in one string
q = ("SELECT * FROM albums WHERE owner = '" + user
     + "' AND id = '" + album_id + "'")
db.execute(q)

Send an album ID of x' OR id='someone-elses and the owner check disappears, because AND binds tighter than OR. The developer didn’t ignore advice. The API takes a string, and a string carries no record of where its characters came from.

You can’t add a runtime check for that, since the information is gone. So the condition is tightened into something a type can carry: the query text consists only of constants written by the developer.

# Safe: the database wrapper accepts only a TrustedSql, and a TrustedSql
# can only be built from a string literal. Values go in separately.
q = TrustedSql("SELECT * FROM albums WHERE owner = ? AND id = ?")
db.query(q, user, album_id)

The attacker’s text now arrives as a bound value and is compared as data. Concatenating input into the query produces something that is not a constant, the API rejects it, and the build fails. The vulnerable program can no longer be expressed. The web equivalent is templates that escape automatically for each context, typed HTML values, and the browser’s Trusted Types standard, which refuses plain strings at dangerous sinks.

Why it scales

Most of a codebase becomes plain safe code with no risky operations at all. A small, stable set of safe abstractions holds every one of them. Experts review those in isolation. The compiler’s type checker then verifies, across the entire codebase and on every build, that nothing reaches a risky operation except through a safe interface.

The chapter places this on a spectrum. Testing samples behaviour. Formal verification proves a property for all behaviour and is so costly it is used mainly for cryptography, protocols and microkernels. Safe Coding sits between: an expert argument about one small module, extended mechanically to every program by the type checker. It captures much of the benefit of formal methods at a fraction of the cost.

Tink: the same pattern for cryptographyUsing a cipher correctly means choosing an algorithm, mode, key size, nonce and padding, and one quiet mistake voids the protection while every test still passes. Google’s Tink library offers operations named for what you want, such as authenticated encryption or digital signatures, with safe choices built in, dangerous options absent and key management included. A developer can encrypt correctly without knowing what a nonce is. People adopt it mostly because it is the easiest correct option, which is the ergonomics point in one example.

Making it stick

  • Build visibility rules restrict who may call the raw risky API.
  • Narrow static checks. Earlier, static analysis hunted an open-ended class of bugs and was unreliable. Here it checks one decidable property, such as “is this argument a compile-time constant?”, so it has no false negatives. Its only failure is being conservative, and that is resolved openly: use the safe pattern or get an expert-reviewed exception.
  • Conformance rules let a codebase declare, and have verified, statements like “no direct DOM manipulation”.
  • Ergonomics. The safe API must be at least as easy as the risky one and cover what people really need. Otherwise they queue for exceptions or find workarounds.

What adoption costs

A small team maintaining safe abstractions can support thousands of developers. Existing code is the expensive part, and the Android numbers show a way through: writing new code in memory-safe languages took memory-safety bugs from over 70% of the total to 24% in six years, without rewriting the old code. Old code’s bugs get found and fixed over time, so new defects concentrate in new code. Stop adding unsafe code and the rate falls.

Plan for an exception process with tracked debt, and use sandboxing to make exceptions cheaper to grant. Count one recurring cost honestly: mandating an API reduces teams’ options and commits the platform team to keeping that API current.

Memory safety

Between 60% and 70% of high-severity vulnerabilities in large C and C++ codebases are memory-safety bugs. Choosing a memory-safe language is the most dependable fix and one of the most consequential platform decisions. The guarantee holds only for the safe subset, so escape hatches (Rust’s unsafe, native extensions) must stay rare, explicit and reviewed. Hardened C++ reduces some bug classes at lower cost and does not give the full guarantee.

Identity and insiders

  • Identity: single sign-on for people, platform-managed identities for workloads, identity carried through every service call.
  • Authentication: frameworks verify credentials before application code runs; mutual TLS between services, handled by the mesh.

The Safe Coding idea applied to configuration. Nobody can hold every permission, network rule and option in their head, and a misconfiguration is as dangerous as a code bug.

Measuring the guarantees

A proxy watching traffic can’t tell whether a response was built by a safe template or whether a CSRF check ran. So Google’s frameworks emit security telemetry themselves, on every request: was the safe templating system used, did CSRF validation run, which code path served this. A central system collects and aggregates it. If a service stops emitting a signal, perhaps because an upgrade broke a check, someone is alerted.

This changes what security monitoring can claim. Traditional monitoring detects the presence of problems, which is never complete. Measuring the presence of structural guarantees gives strong evidence of the absence of entire classes of defect.

Reliability: the platform turns SRE into a default

The big picture

Reliability means meeting what users expect, consistently and correctly. Uptime is one part of it. A system can be up and too slow to use, or up and returning wrong data. The chapter lists six dimensions: availability, latency, correctness, freshness, durability and throughput. For AI services, correctness becomes probabilistic.

The central sentence of the chapter is this one, paraphrased: SRE provides the blueprint for reliability, and platform engineering provides the machinery that produces reliable services at scale. Asking every team to learn and apply SRE practices on their own doesn’t scale. The platform’s job is to capture those practices as code and APIs, so a team inherits them by using the standard path.

The economics: local savings, global costs

Feature teams are rewarded for shipping. That is a local measure. When a team skips resilience work to ship sooner, the cost doesn’t disappear. It moves to the organisation as cascading failures, firefighting and cross-team incident response. Economists call that an externality.

The chapter is kind about motive: teams rarely decide to trade reliability for speed. They assume everything will be fine. It names the result bluntly, though. Reliability becomes an unfunded local mandate, and everyone suffers.

A platform internalises the cost. Building an automated canary analysis service might take four engineers several months. After that, no product team writes or watches its own deployment checks, and the investment typically pays back within a year.

Canary analysis as a platform gateGoogle’s service compares a new release’s telemetry (latency, errors, resource use) statistically against the version already running. It sits in the deployment platform as an automatic gate. A significant regression stops the rollout and triggers a rollback. Nobody stares at a dashboard during a deploy.

Two details in that table are worth saying aloud. Capacity changes are rolled out through the same progressive pipeline and canary checks as software, so an infrastructure change is as safe as a release. And the platform owns the how of deployment while the developer owns the what.

Break-glass, done properlyStrict rules sometimes have to be bypassed to restore service. Suppose only signed, scanned images may deploy, and an emergency fix can’t wait for the full pipeline. A break-glass mechanism lets responders override the policy with an explicit marker on the deployment. The important part is what happens next: the override is written to the audit log, a high-priority alert goes to security and operations, and the record is permanent for review afterwards. It is a platform feature with accountability, never a silent bypass.

Observability and the human

The chapter describes an incident as a joint cognitive system: people and tools share a working memory, with operators relying on the system to show them what is happening. When every service’s metrics have the same names and every dashboard the same layout, responders work from muscle memory and spend their attention on the problem. When each service is different, they spend the first critical minutes decoding the environment.

For AI and agent workloads the golden signals aren’t enough. Add model freshness, version skew, data quality and probabilistic correctness, and for agents: tokens used, end-to-end latency of an invocation, and which external tools were called and how often.

The platform as institutional memory

If several teams have outages from the same kind of misconfiguration, the answer is not retraining or a documentation update. Change the platform: add validation, change the default, or remove the setting altogether. That is how an organisation stops having the same outage.

A related feature at Google: any change in source control can be marked as a bad change, and the deployment system then refuses every rollout that contains it.

The reliability of the platform itself

Centralising makes the platform the most critical system in the company. If the deployment system is down, nobody can release, and nobody can roll back either. If monitoring is down, everyone is blind. A reliable platform multiplies stability for everything on it. An unreliable one aggregates risk.

So platform teams must apply SRE to themselves, and to a higher standard than the applications they host.

  • Strict, published SLOs for the platform: build success rate, deployment latency, monitoring ingestion lag.
  • Defence in depth: no internal single points of failure, and graceful degradation.
  • Conservative rollouts with fast rollback, because a platform bug has the whole organisation as its blast radius.
  • Disaster practice for the case where the platform is the thing that is down.

Sublinear scaleWithout a platform, operational effort grows in step with the number of services. With one, the cost of each capability is paid once and each fix lands everywhere.

Say it in an interview

Interview practice

Answer aloud for two minutes before you open the model answer.

How do you decide which reliability features go into the platform and which stay with product teams?

Show a model answer

I use three tests. The first is blast radius: if one team getting it wrong can hurt other teams, it belongs in the platform. Retry behaviour is the classic case. A service that retries hard without backoff can take down a shared database, so the client library should enforce backoff and a retry budget for everyone.

The second is complexity. If doing it well needs rare expertise, such as statistical canary analysis or multi-region failover, building it once and offering it as a service is cheaper than having twenty teams each build a worse version.

The third is the cost of a mistake. If the worst outcome is minor, I leave the team free to choose. If it is catastrophic or irreversible, such as draining all regions or deleting the only copy of data, the platform should make it impossible, with a logged break-glass path for genuine emergencies.

What I keep out is business logic. A platform that knows about one product’s special cases becomes rigid and harder for everyone else to understand.

Then I match strictness to risk. A critical service with little error budget gets slow staged rollouts and long bake times automatically. An internal tool gets a fast path. That keeps the safe default from becoming a tax on low-risk work.

Your deployment platform had an outage and nobody could ship or roll back for two hours. What do you change?

Show a model answer

First, the platform needs its own SLOs, stricter than its tenants’, and published: pipeline success rate counting only system-caused failures, time from merge to deployed, and availability of the rollback path as its own objective.

Second, I’d separate the rollback path from the rest. Rolling back to a known-good version should depend on as little as possible: no build step, no external registry call that can fail, cached artifacts. I’d test it with fault injection.

Third, the platform should be rolled out more conservatively than anything it deploys: staged across cells or regions with bake time, so a platform bug hits a fraction of teams first.

Fourth, a break-glass route for when the platform is down: a documented, audited manual deployment that responders have practised.

And I’d run the postmortem with the same question we ask of product teams: what made this failure possible, and which default should change so it can’t recur?

Developer experience: platforms nobody uses are worth nothing

You will learn

  • What “broccoli” means in this book

  • How to cultivate it, and how to measure it

The big picture

Organisations spend heavily on internal platforms that are robust, scalable and technically impressive, and then watch adoption stall. Developers keep their old scripts or build unofficial tools around the official one. The platform team is frustrated that nobody appreciates what they built. Developers feel burdened by tools that make their work harder.

The chapter calls this the platform paradox. Its explanation is that a platform succeeds or fails less on what it technically provides than on what it is like to use.

“Build it and they will come”

This is the belief that a technically superior platform attracts users by itself. It misreads developers in three ways.

It ignores their workflowPeople have tuned how they work. A tool that makes them abandon that feels like an implant, not an extension.
It costs too much attentionIf understanding the platform takes more thought than the business problem, people look for an easier route.
The payoff is too far awayDevelopers are measured on what ships now. A steep learning curve with long-term benefits loses to a simple fix today, and that is a rational choice.
The broccoli problemAn old saying inside Google went roughly: things that should be easy are medium-hard here, and things that should be impossible are also medium-hard. An engineer made a short cartoon, known as the “Broccoli Man” video, in which someone only wants to serve five terabytes of data and is buried in process. In this book, broccoli means paying the setup and running costs of a billion-user system for something small: an internal tool, a prototype, a dashboard. It doesn’t come from incompetence. It comes from putting an application on the wrong kind of platform. The chapter’s example: three days to bring up a “hello world” server because the platform demands cluster names and outdated access settings, for a task that is a one-click form elsewhere.

The costs go beyond wasted investment: lower productivity and morale, burnout, less experimentation, and unofficial tooling that adds debt and security risk.

What developer experience is

The chapter’s definition, in my words: developer experience is everything a developer encounters, perceives and feels when dealing with a platform, its tools and the team behind it. It is a technical discipline about removing friction, and has little to do with keeping people cheerful.

Feedback loopsHow fast and how clear the response to an action is. Test times, build times, the quality of an error message.
Cognitive loadHow much thinking a task needs. Driven by complexity, the number of tools, and the quality of abstractions.
Flow stateUninterrupted, absorbed work. Broken by waiting, context switches, external dependencies and toil.

This settles the old argument about clicking in a console versus automating. A good console is the best way to explore and understand a system’s state. The API underneath means the same action can be scripted when it becomes routine. People move from exploring by hand to automation without changing tools.

Going deeper the business case, how to cultivate it, and what to measure

Why it is a business matter

  • Productivity. The best-performing organisations in the DORA research aren’t working longer hours. Their tools remove friction, so lead times are hours, not weeks. One survey cited in the chapter found developers spend more than 15 hours a week on tasks other than writing application code.
  • People. Satisfaction with tools tracks team performance and lower burnout. An employee recommendation score measures past friction, which makes a falling score an early warning of future productivity loss. Good tooling also helps hiring and retention.
  • Innovation. If trying an idea takes minutes, people try ideas. That is psychological safety in practical form.
  • Risk. A service template that arrives with security scanning, structured logging and monitoring already wired makes the right way the easy way. Developers meet complex standards without becoming experts in them. Investing in experience is investing in risk management, which matters most in regulated industries.

Jobs to be done

The value proposition comes from the job a developer is trying to get done, which is rarely the feature they ask for. “I need a deploy button” is a request. “I need to get my change to production quickly and with confidence” is the job.

The chapter’s example: a developer asks for a dashboard of build logs. Going deeper the product manager, the toolkit, the lifecycle and the scorecard

The platform product manager

A product manager for something that earns no revenue can look like a luxury. The chapter argues the role is the owner of the developer experience and the link between what the platform can do and what the organisation needs.

  • Shape the vision. Where is the platform going, and why does that matter to developers and the business?
  • Be the customer’s voice in every planning and prioritisation discussion.
  • Translate in both directions. Developers’ needs into terms leaders use, such as delivery metrics and time to market; leaders’ goals into a roadmap engineers can build.
  • Prioritise. Make the trade-offs between user value, feasibility and business impact.

They do it with engineering and design from the first day. Throwing requirements over a wall is named as an antipattern. If there is no dedicated person, the engineering manager or tech lead wears the hat, and must protect time for talking to users, or delivery pressure will eat it.

Discovery habits

  • Talk to developers regularly, as open conversations about their work. These are not pitches.
  • Run journey-mapping workshops on one workflow, such as onboarding a new service. Sticky notes on a wall expose hidden friction fast.

The purpose is to avoid the most common failure: an elegant platform that solves a problem nobody has.

Build, buy or partner

  1. Is this a core competency or a competitive advantage? Then build. A trading firm builds its own low-latency trading system.
  2. Does something existing solve 80% of it? Then lean towards buying or partnering. The lifetime cost of building and maintaining in-house is usually far more than the licence.
  3. Can you adopt an off-the-shelf product and spend your engineering on the unique last mile? Often the most practical answer. Less engineering, though never none.

The MVP you run by hand

Want to build a deployment pipeline? Write no code yet. Find a friendly team and do their deployments for them for two weeks: run the scripts, provision the infrastructure, watch the logs. It doesn’t scale and isn’t meant to. It shows you every edge case and last-mile problem a whiteboard would miss. You are looking for a problem so basic that developers hit it several times a day. Write code only after a handful of hand-served teams are happy.

From golden path to town square

A mature platform is less like one road and more like a town square. The platform team provides the paving, lighting and plumbing, meaning reliability, security and core APIs, and keeps the main roads that take 80% of people where they need to go. Around the square, other teams build their own shops through plugins, extension points and contribution models. The community solves its long-tail problems and the platform team stays on the core.

“Adoption is voluntary, even when it’s mandatory”The chapter’s best line. You can order people to use a platform. You can’t order them to use it well. A top-down mandate without a product worth choosing gets obedience to the letter and workarounds in spirit.

Deprecation is a launch in reverse

  1. A plan: a timeline, a migration path and a reason people will accept.
  2. Communication, early and repeated, in several channels.
  3. Tooling and support. Automate the migration where you can.
  4. A final off switch. Until the old system is turned off, you haven’t collected the benefit.

To find out who still depends on an old service, run a brownout: break it deliberately for a short time and see who notices.

Platforms for AIInfrastructure, abstractions and workflows for the whole life of a model. Usually called MLOps.

The two feed each other. A well-abstracted platform is what lets an organisation use AI at scale, and the intelligence that produces is then turned on the platform. The chapter is written from Google’s position: the same investments that made Search and Gmail possible, in cluster management, distributed storage and custom accelerators, are what made training very large models possible.

A naming pointThe chapter refers to Google Cloud’s managed AI service as “Agent Platform”, with components for pipelines, a feature store, a model registry and experiment tracking. I use generic names here, since the ideas apply on any cloud.

Why AI needs its own platform

An AI system is more than code. It is code, data and model configuration bound together, and that three-way coupling multiplies complexity. In ordinary software a person writes the logic. In machine learning the logic is learned from data.

The chapter’s instruction is the abstraction imperative: servers were abstracted into virtual machines and containers to free application developers, and now accelerators, data pipelines and training workflows must be abstracted to free ML engineers.

Going deeper quality attributes for AI, the middle layer, and AIOps

The quality attributes, applied to AI

Efficiency

Training one large model can use enormous amounts of energy and compute. Managing accelerators centrally lets the platform scale predictively and right-size from usage history, where teams would otherwise hold expensive hardware idle. And an improvement to a shared piece, such as the data-loading library or the serving protocol, reaches every AI team at once. The chapter calls it a flywheel.

Reliability: the silent failure

In ordinary software, a fast “200 OK” usually means the service works. A model endpoint can return a fast 200 with an answer that is wrong or harmful. So model and data quality become first-class reliability signals.

SignalWhat can go wrongExample
Model freshnessA model starts ageing the moment it ships, because the world movesA stale fraud model is a reliability incident even while the API is healthy
Data qualityInputs drift away from what the model was trained on. Different from integrity, which asks whether a file is corruptA fraud model tuned on transactions of $10 to $500 starts receiving $5,000 ones
Probabilistic correctnessOutputs aren’t deterministic, so you can’t assert on themUse confidence thresholds and safety filters, and block low-confidence answers

That last row contains a trade worth naming: the platform chooses to refuse an answer, giving up some availability, to avoid a confident wrong one. It is fail closed, applied to a model.

Safety and security

  • Supply chain. Scan containers, and deploy only signed and verified models.
  • Protecting the weights. Model weights are often core intellectual property. Confidential computing keeps data and models encrypted while in use, shielding them even from platform operators and the infrastructure provider.
  • Adversarial defence. Input and output guardrails deployed as gateways in the infrastructure, working independently of the model, to catch prompt injection, jailbreak attempts and toxic output.
  • Safe change. Canary a new model version on a fraction of traffic and roll back automatically if metrics fall.

The middle layer

Between feature teams and the AI backends sits integration glue. Done badly it slows everyone down or gets ignored. Done well it saves feature teams from repeating integration work and lets the backends scale without their effort growing in step. Because AI changes so fast, the chapter sets three rules for it.

  1. Iterate quickly. Prefer small prototypes, with a plan to harden the parts that prove themselves.
  2. Velocity first. The layer exists to remove overhead. Any part that slows teams without a clear benefit destroys its own value.
  3. Expect pivots. Deliver something partial early. A command-line tool this month can beat a polished web application next quarter, and is easier to change.

ML engineers are also a distinct kind of customer. Their work is iterative and lives in notebooks, so offer SDKs before command lines, and visual tools for lineage, training curves and comparing experiments.

AI for platforms

Modern platforms produce more telemetry than people can read. The chapter describes AIOps as the highest-leverage form of shifting down: expert operational knowledge encoded into tools that watch, predict and act.

  1. Make sense of the noiseOne failure can set off thousands of alerts across dependent services. Models correlate them, narrow down the source and produce one targeted alert.
  2. PredictLearn normal behaviour in place of fixed thresholds. Catch memory leaks and failing hardware early, and forecast capacity shortfalls in time to provision.
  3. Assist the operatorAsk in plain language what caused a latency spike. Have incident chat summarised and the postmortem drafted. Get a probable cause and a suggested playbook, with a person still deciding.
  4. ActSchedule workloads against performance, cost and energy together. Restart a stuck process or roll back safely without waiting. Scale ahead of demand using history and upstream traffic.

Agentic platforms

Platforms now have a second kind of user. Consoles, APIs and SDKs serve people. Agents need structured, intent-based APIs and tool definitions they can discover and reason about.

When agents do the operating, the platform’s role moves from supplying tools to supplying controls and policies. An autonomous agent may wander from what was intended, so the platform has to be the physics of the environment: the security, compliance and reliability limits that hold whatever the agent tries.

Back to pace layersThe chapter closes with the layers model from lesson 2. AI applications are the fast layer, changing constantly. The platform is the slow, stabilising layer. It absorbs the shocks and gradually takes in what has proven itself above.
Say it in an interview

There are two jobs. One is building the platform AI teams need: hide the accelerators, give them standard pipelines, a feature store and a model registry, and treat freshness, drift and confidence as reliability signals, because a model can return a 200 and be wrong. The other is using AI to run the platform: correlate alerts, predict problems, assist responders and, with guardrails, act. In both, the platform’s part is the controls that hold regardless of what a model or agent does.

On AWSFor the ML path: SageMaker Pipelines for standard workflows, Feature Store against training-serving skew, Model Registry with approval status as the source of truth, managed training with checkpointing and Spot for fault-tolerant jobs, and Model Monitor for data and quality drift. For generative workloads, Bedrock Guardrails act as the input and output gateway independent of the model, and Nitro Enclaves or confidential instances protect weights in use. Sign model artifacts and gate deployment on the signature, as with any other binary. For AIOps: CloudWatch anomaly detection and investigations, DevOps Guru for correlated insights, predictive scaling, and agents whose permissions are bounded by IAM and Service Control Policies so the limits hold whatever the agent decides.

Education: why teaching belongs to the platform team

You will learn
  • Why a culture of learning is the root of engineering performance
  • What a computer science degree can’t cover
  • Why education fits the platform organisation, and how to staff it
  • What AI changes: documentation, specification and verification

The big picture

The chapter opens with a rule from its author that is worth memorising: if something is accidentally complex, simplify it; if it is inherently complex, educate.

Its argument has three steps. Engineering performance comes from culture. The root of good culture is learning and growth. And somebody has to be responsible for the learning that can’t be left to chance, which is most naturally the platform organisation.

A culture of learning

Management thinkers, academic researchers, Google’s own team studies and the DORA research all point the same way: culture is at the root of performance. The culture they describe is psychological safety, where it is safe to be wrong, safe not to know, and safe to make an honest mistake.

The chapter makes a sharper point than “people work better when they aren’t afraid”. Safety is what lets information move and makes learning valued.

What psychological safety does not excuseA sceptic once told the author that it just lets people off for failing. His answer: it excuses honest, novel mistakes. Repeating the same error, or doing something plainly careless, should still count against you. The aim is that an organisation learns each hard lesson once, not once per engineer because everyone hides their failures.

Who gets the next task?

An old model from the 1970s, the “surgical team”, gives the critical work to the best engineer and has everyone else support them. The chapter explains why that no longer fits, in game-theory terms. It was a strategy for a single game: assemble a team, build one system, disband. Modern engineering is an endless stream of projects with people moving between teams.

Single gameGive everything to your best person
→
Endless gameGive the next task to the most junior engineer who can do it with some guidance

The second rule values growth, and frees senior people for new problems and mentoring.

Brittle productivity

Research by Cat Hicks describes what happens without a learning culture. Only submitted code counts. Investigating, understanding and explaining look like waste. Teaching isn’t rewarded. Engineers come to define productivity as effort, hours and solitary work, which the chapter calls performative hustle. Her terms are learning debt and brittle productivity.

It suggests a reframing borrowed from her: stop saying “developer productivity” and say “developer problem solving”. That makes it obvious that skills and understanding are the thing to build.

Documentation is where this shows. It is the one factor in the DORA research that may matter even more than team culture, and it is the most visible, measurable sign of whether a team values the group’s output over individual output.

A thought experimentWhich team would you rather lead? One that is forbidden from using any IDE or AI assistant and keeps everything else. Or one with every modern tool that can never learn anything beyond what its members were taught at university. Tools help. Without learning, engineering stops.
Going deeper what degrees miss, how to organise education, and AI

Why universities can’t do it for us

  • The field is young. Certification works for disciplines that are centuries old. Software engineering is about fifty years old and still finding its best practices.
  • There aren’t enough hours. All the lectures, labs and homework in a computing degree add up to less than 18 months of full-time work, and less than a third of the popular “10,000 hours to mastery” figure.
  • The content is skewed. Students learn to implement a linked list more than they learn when to use one.
  • Software engineering is programming plus time plus people. Version control, testing and design patterns exist to cope with change over time and with other engineers. A course can’t make either of those real.
  • Student teams aren’t teams. Real teams mix experience levels, have authority structures and face a stream of work.
  • Nobody reads the code. Enrolment in the US grew about fivefold in 15 years and teaching staff didn’t, so early courses are graded automatically on whether the output is right. Some graduates have never had a person comment on their code.

At Google a new hire’s first code review is treated as a rite of passage. It teaches the difference between “it seems to work” and “it is done to a quality we can afford to maintain”.

Why education belongs with the platform

A company’s business strategy is to diversify into many products, each with its own specialists. Its infrastructure strategy should be to keep one platform. So the platform organisation is the one part of engineering that sees the whole enterprise and works on what all teams share.

  • Expertise accumulates there. Being the Java expert on a team of ten is one thing. Being the person 10,000 Java engineers bring their questions to is another. That depth is learned from answering the same questions and collecting the edge cases.
  • The platform chooses what is easy. If it has made one way standard, it owes people an explanation of that way. Managing the community actively beats hoping good practice spreads by itself.
  • The missions match. Education increases engineering capacity and reduces waste, misuse and misconfiguration of expensive systems. That is the platform’s purpose too.

How to staff it

OptionVerdict
Everyone teaches, with targetsNo. Good educators are rare, and the wrong person in that role does harm
A separate education departmentNo. It drifts away from current practice and real problems
MatrixedYes. On each component team, a few engineers who like to write or teach get part of their time for community support, writing up best practice, and building and delivering training. Reward it

The chapter compares this to how SRE treats toil. When you are tired of answering the same question, write the essay, record the talk or build the class. Done well, education reduces the support load the same way automation reduces operational load.

Keep the scope tied to what the platform supports. A company standardised on Go, Kubernetes and a managed Git service should teach those, and should not branch into unrelated topics. For general material, curate instead of producing. Popularity is a poor guide: studies in physics education found that students’ own rating of how much they learned tracks how much they liked the instructor, and has little to do with measured learning.

Education and AI

The chapter quotes the 2025 DORA finding that AI is an amplifier. It magnifies the strengths of organisations that work well and the dysfunction of those that don’t. The returns come from the underlying system: the quality of the internal platform, clear workflows, aligned teams.

Clean up your documentationYour tools are internal, so a general model doesn’t know them. You point it at your docs. If they are current, the assistant is useful. If they are stale, it repeats stale answers and invents the rest. AI can’t tell an outdated page from a current one.
Teach specificationWriting new code is no longer the bottleneck. Describing precisely what is needed is: requirements, contracts, constraints, architecture.
Teach verificationThe other bottleneck is checking that the result is fit for purpose: reading code, reviewing, testing, thinking critically.

Both onboarding and retraining need this. The chapter’s closing view is that software engineering can’t really be separated from education, and AI has made a long-postponed investment urgent.

Say it in an interview

I think of education as part of the platform’s job. The platform team sees every team’s problems and decides what the standard way is, so it should also explain that way. I’d give a few engineers on each platform component real time for support, writing and teaching, and I’d treat repeated questions as toil to automate with documentation. With AI in the picture it matters more: assistants answer from our docs, and the scarce skills are now specifying what we want and verifying what we got.

On AWSMake documentation part of the golden path: every template ships a README, a runbook and architecture notes in the repository, rendered in the developer portal. Keep them current with ownership metadata and a staleness check in CI, because that corpus is what an internal assistant (Amazon Q Business or a Bedrock knowledge base) will answer from. Scope training to what the platform supports, and curate AWS Skill Builder paths instead of writing general cloud courses yourself. Use game days and Well-Architected reviews as teaching moments, not audits.

Show a model answer

I’d start from the finding that AI amplifies whatever is already there. If our platform, workflows and documentation are in good shape, assistants will help a lot. If they aren’t, we’ll produce confusion faster. So part of the rollout is fixing the foundations.

First, documentation. Our internal tools aren’t in any public model’s training, so the assistant will answer from our docs. I’d audit the most-used pages, delete or fix the stale ones, give every page an owner and a review date, and make the content structured enough for a machine to use.

Second, guardrails in the platform, so generated code is safe by construction: safe APIs it must compile against, signed builds, policy checks at deploy. Then review doesn’t have to catch everything.

Third, training, aimed at the new bottlenecks. Writing code is cheap now. Specifying precisely what we want and verifying what comes back are the scarce skills, so I’d teach requirements and contracts, code reading, review and testing, to juniors and seniors alike.

Fourth, I’d protect how juniors learn. If the assistant does every task a junior would have learned from, we’ll have no seniors in five years. I’d keep giving juniors real tasks with guidance and make explaining the result part of review.

I’d measure delivery metrics and satisfaction before and after, by team, and expect the gain to be largest where the foundations were strongest.

Executive summary

The short version of both books, and where they meet. S8 means SRE chapter 8; P15 means platform book chapter 15 in its final numbering. The references are links.

The two books in one paragraph each

Site Reliability Engineering, 2nd Edition describes how to run software so that it is as reliable as its users need and no more. It treats operations as an engineering problem, makes reliability measurable through service level objectives and error budgets, and organises everything else as a pyramid, the hierarchy of production health: culture at the base, then awareness, preparedness, infrastructure design, resilient products and, at the top, platforms that make services reliable by default.

Platform Engineering at Google starts where that pyramid ends. Its claim is that security, reliability and efficiency can’t be delivered by asking every developer to be careful. They have to be moved down into the languages, libraries, pipelines and infrastructure that all code is built on, so that experts solve each problem once and every team inherits the result. Only ten of its thirty-one planned chapters are published.

What the SRE book argues

  • 100% is the wrong target. Pick a target from what users need, and treat the gap as a budget to spend on change. (S2, S8)
  • Culture comes first. Blameless learning, psychological safety and sustainable on-call are the base of the pyramid, not a soft extra. (S1, S4)
  • Measure from the user’s side. SLOs belong to user journeys, alerts fire on how fast the budget burns, and low-traffic services need statistics, not thresholds. (S8, S9)
  • Mitigate before you understand. Clear roles, generic mitigations and practised emergency modes shorten incidents more than better diagnosis does. (S10, S17)
  • Accidents are control problems. STPA and CAST replace the hunt for a root cause with the question of why the system allowed the unsafe action. (S11, S12)
  • Changes cause most outages. So test configuration like code, roll out progressively, compare a canary with a control, and roll back automatically. (S14, S17)
  • Shift down. The durable fix for a class of failure is a platform that makes it impossible or handles it by default. (S18, S23)
  • AI cuts both ways. AI systems need quality as a reliability signal, and AI operators need autonomy that is earned, bounded and reversible. (S19–S21)

What the platform book argues

  • Shift down, not only left. Shifting left finds a problem earlier and leaves it with the developer. Shifting down makes the platform responsible. (P15, P16)
  • Conformity buys leverage. The more an ecosystem limits choice, the more liability it can carry for its users. Make that trade explicit with resources, rights and responsibilities. (P12)
  • Coupling is a decision. It buys efficiency, performance, atomicity and traceability and costs reuse, extensibility, robustness and choice. The danger is coupling nobody chose. (P9)
  • Layers move at different speeds. Experiments and products are fast; infrastructure, directives, culture and law are slow. Fast layers propose and slow layers decide. (P10)
  • If it isn’t secure, it shouldn’t compile. Wrap each risky operation in a safe abstraction so the vulnerable program can’t be written, then carry the same idea into builds and deployment. (P15)
  • SRE is the blueprint; the platform is the machinery. Put into the platform whatever has a wide blast radius, needs rare expertise or fails catastrophically. (P16)
  • One optimisation, every team. Shared foundations let you see the whole fleet and fix the biggest consumer once, on by default. (P14)
  • A platform nobody uses is worth nothing. Design the experience, run the platform as a product, and measure adopters against non-adopters. (P18, P19)
  • AI amplifies what is already there. Good platforms and current documentation make assistants useful; poor ones make the mess arrive faster. (P22, P24)
